# Proposed write-up passage (for the owner's article; not applied to it)

Once the library could assemble a Stellaris helium loop with the ARIES Brayton chain and price it, the natural question was how to run it. The design point, 2,500 kg/s of helium at an equal stage pressure ratio of 1.52, nets 427 MW. Earlier cases had shown that lowering the pressure ratio raised the calculated electricity while quietly leaving part of the 773 K source heat unremoved, so the study asked the question fairly: over an 8 × 16 grid of flow and ratio, with every equipment and thermal check evaluated and every failed case kept on the map, which points actually pass, and what stops the rest?

The answer has a shape. At every flow there is a lowest ratio below which the cycle can no longer take all the heat: the compressor exit and the recuperator return the helium to the heater too hot for the 50 MW/K exchanger to carry the source's heat across the remaining temperature difference. The net electricity keeps rising right up to that boundary and peaks one step past it, on the failing side. The best that passes every check is a band, 2,250 kg/s at ratio 1.52 or 2,750 kg/s at 1.375, about 595 MW, 40 % more than the design point, with the conditional LCOE falling 29 % because the same fixed costs are spread over more energy. The electricity-only best, 620 MW at 2,500 kg/s and 1.425, leaves 8 MW of source heat unremoved and is not a steady operating point. Above the band the compressor rating binds, and beyond 3,000 kg/s the net itself goes to zero as the turbine inlet falls.

Two things this study does not say are as important as what it does. The machine efficiencies are fixed numbers with no off-design map; shifting them moves the boundary by one grid step, so the operating claim is scoped near the design flow, where a 2,500 kg/s / 1.45 point passes under every efficiency tested. And the improvement is bounded by hardware, not by cleverness in operation: a 50 % larger exchanger, priced through the same purchase law, moves the boundary down and buys another 74 MW for under a dollar per megawatt-hour. The inventory the ARIES source selected supports none of these points at all, which is the study's other lesson: choose the equipment before the sweep, price what was chosen, and let a too-small rating stay a failed case rather than resize it to fit.
