# Proposed write-up passage (for the owner's article; not applied to it)

Once the library could assemble a Stellaris helium loop with the ARIES Brayton chain and price it, the natural question was how to run it. The design point, 2,500 kg/s of helium at an equal stage pressure ratio of 1.52, nets 427 MW. Earlier cases had shown that lowering the pressure ratio raised the calculated electricity while quietly leaving part of the 773 K source heat unremoved, so the study asked the question fairly: over an 8 × 16 grid of flow and ratio, with every equipment and thermal check evaluated and every failed case kept on the map, which points actually pass, and what stops the rest?

The answer has a shape. At every flow there is a lowest ratio below which the cycle can no longer take all the heat: the compressor exit and the recuperator return the helium to the heater too hot for the 50 MW/K exchanger to carry the source's heat across the remaining temperature difference. The net electricity keeps rising right up to that boundary and peaks one step past it, on the failing side. The best that passes every check is a band, 2,250 kg/s at ratio 1.52 or 2,750 kg/s at 1.375, about 595 MW: operating choices alone, with the existing equipment, give 40 % more electricity than the design point, and the nonfuel cost of electricity falls by 29 % because the same plant costs are spread over more energy. (The total conditional figure falls further, from 1,559 to 1,113 USD per megawatt-hour, but almost all of that is an assumed tritium purchase price spread over more electricity, and it does not change which points are best.) The electricity-only best, 620 MW at 2,500 kg/s and 1.425, leaves 8 MW of source heat unremoved and is not a steady operating point. Above the band the compressor rating binds, and beyond 3,000 kg/s the net itself goes to zero as the turbine inlet falls.

Three things this study does not say are as important as what it does. The machine efficiencies are fixed numbers with no off-design map; shifting them moves the boundary by one grid step, so the operating claim is scoped near the design flow, where a 2,500 kg/s / 1.45 point passes under every efficiency tested. Further improvement is limited by exchanger capacity: a 50 % larger exchanger, priced through the same purchase law, moves the boundary down and buys another 74 MW for under a dollar per megawatt-hour of added capital contribution. And the loop's own boundary condition, that the helium must come back from the exchanger at the temperature the circulator needs to restore the 300 °C blanket inlet, is stated in the loop definition but was never checked in the assembly: at the best points the computed return is within a degree to nine degrees of it, at the design point it is 49 degrees too cold, so the points nearest the heat-removal boundary are also the most consistent circulating loops. The inventory the ARIES source selected supports none of these points at all, which is the study's other lesson: choose the equipment before the sweep, price what was chosen, and let a too-small rating stay a failed case rather than resize it to fit.
