# Proposed write-up passage (for the owner's article; not applied to it)

Once the library could assemble a Stellaris helium loop with the ARIES Brayton chain and price it, the natural question was how to run it. The design point, 2,500 kg/s of helium at an equal stage pressure ratio of 1.52, nets 427 MW. Earlier cases had shown that lowering the pressure ratio raised the calculated electricity while quietly leaving part of the 773 K source heat unremoved, so the study asked the question fairly: over a grid of flow and ratio, with every equipment and thermal check evaluated and every failed case kept on the map, which points actually pass, and what stops the rest?

The first pass found the shape but not the point. At every flow there is a lowest ratio below which the cycle can no longer take all the heat, and the electricity keeps rising right up to it. What the first pass missed was a requirement written in the loop's own definition: the helium has to come back from the exchanger at the temperature the circulator needs to restore the 300 °C blanket inlet, and nothing in the assembly checked it. Once that relationship was modeled, as a bypass that routes around the exchanger whatever primary flow it does not need, with the exchanger's reduced-flow performance computed rather than assumed, the picture sharpened. The design point turns out to be over-provisioned on its exchanger: it holds its loop balance only by bypassing 31 % of the flow. The consistent operating points are where the exchanger is exactly matched to its duty, one ratio for each flow. Among the tested points the design flow does best: 2,500 kg/s at a stage ratio of 1.43 nets 621 MW, with 2,750 kg/s only 3 MW behind, inside the study's materiality, so the result is a band of flows rather than a single optimum. That is 45 % more than the design point, with the nonfuel cost of electricity falling by 31 % because the same plant costs are spread over more energy. It is a conditional model result on the same selected major equipment: the design point holds its loop balance only through a modeled 31 % bypass whose hardware, cost and pressure loss are not in the model, and the matched point needs none. (The total conditional figure falls further, from 1,559 to 1,072 USD per megawatt-hour, but almost all of that is an assumed tritium purchase price spread over more electricity, and it does not change which point is best.)

Three things this study does not say are as important as what it does. The machine efficiencies are fixed numbers with no off-design map; shifting them moves the matched ratio by about a grid step. The 621 MW point sits exactly at the exchanger limit with no margin; a step inside it, 616 MW with about 2 % bypass, buys a margin for 5 MW. Neither is an operating recommendation: that would need the machine maps and the bypass control analysis the model does not have, and the bypass limit itself is left unspecified. Further improvement is limited by exchanger capacity: a 50 % larger exchanger, priced through the same purchase law, adds another 51 MW for under a dollar per megawatt-hour of added capital contribution. And the inventory the ARIES source selected supports none of these points at all, which is the study's other lesson: choose the equipment before the sweep, price what was chosen, and let a too-small rating stay a failed case rather than resize it to fit.
