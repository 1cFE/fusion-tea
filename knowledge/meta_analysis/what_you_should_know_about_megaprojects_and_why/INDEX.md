## 11 **The "Break-Fix Model" of Megaproject Management**
**Lines:** 153-162

Section 11 introduces the "megaprojects paradox" (rising demand despite 70 years of unchanged poor performance) and defines the "break-fix model" — where projects launch on misinformation, inevitably break via cost/schedule overruns, and get reorganized at stakeholder expense due to lock-in and escalation, with the proposed remedy being proper front-end management.

## 12 **Hirschman's Hiding Hand, Revisited**
**Lines:** 163-202

Critiques Hirschman's "Hiding Hand" principle and Sawyer's "creative error" concept—the idea that ignorance of true project costs is beneficial because it enables ambitious projects to be started and triggers human creativity to overcome unforeseen difficulties. Argues these theories rest on small biased samples (11 and 10-15 projects respectively), are disproved by Kahneman and Tversky's findings that optimism bias inflates both cost and benefit estimates simultaneously, and uses the Sydney Opera House cost overrun (which destroyed architect Jørn Utzon's career) to illustrate the hidden costs of "creative error" megaproject management.

## 13 **Survival of the Unfittest**
**Lines:** 203-222

Section 13 argues that cost underestimation and benefit overestimation in megaprojects are indefensible on economic, legal, and ethical grounds (citing Sarbanes-Oxley and democratic obligation-to-truth principles), and introduces Flyvbjerg's "survival of the unfittest" / inverted Darwinism concept — where projects with the largest estimation distortions get selected over genuinely viable ones, citing counter-examples like the Guggenheim Bilbao, Paris-Lyon HSR, and London Docklands extension as projects delivered on time and budget.
