### 10.1 Short-diffusion timescales.
**Lines:** 87-98

Section 10.1 covers how short diffusion timescales correlate with technology granularity, presenting evidence that lower unit investment costs enable faster 1-to-50% market share diffusion, that investment risk (measured via capital cost overruns) decreases with project size for granular renewables but increases for large hydro/nuclear/thermal plants, and that learning rates are faster for more granular energy technologies.

## 11 **ESCAPING LOCK-IN**
**Lines:** 99-110

Covers how granular technologies help escape fossil-fuel lock-in through three mechanisms: faster capital stock renewal (shorter technical lifetimes enable quicker turnover), lower technological complexity (fewer components, easier innovation cycles), and system downsizing via end-use efficiency (one unit saved avoids 3.2 units of primary energy). References Wilson et al. (2020) "Granular technologies to accelerate decarbonization" in Science.

## 14 **SUBSTITUTABILITY AND RISKS OF GRANULARITY**
**Lines:** 143-154

Covers substitutability limits between granular and lumpy technologies (e.g., distributed generation vs. centralized plants, with non-substitutable cases like long-haul flights and steel/cement), and identifies three risks of greater granularity: coordination/security challenges from digitalization, higher transaction costs (mitigable via aggregation, standardization, or third-party management), and pollution exposure plus material waste from distributed and short-lived technologies.

## 15 **STANDARDISATION AND LOCK-IN**
**Lines:** 155-164

Covers how standardisation of granular technologies converges variety onto dominant designs to enable mass production, cost reduction, and quality control, while warning of risks like technological lock-in, monopolistic operators from network externalities, and disregard for local context — noting that standardising fundamentals still permits differentiated applications.

## 16 **UNIT AND MANUFACTURING ECONOMIES OF SCALE**
**Lines:** 165-170

Covers unit vs. manufacturing economies of scale as alternative cost-reduction drivers across energy technologies, citing examples where solar PV manufacturing scale-up explained >1/3 of 2001-2012 module cost reductions while coal plant up-scaling explained ~3/4 of US coal cost reductions from 1908-1970.

## 17 **ACCESS TO INFRASTRUCTURE AND SYSTEM INTEGRATION**
**Lines:** 171-178

Discusses how technological turnover times scale from years (technologies) to centuries (infrastructures), citing Wilson et al. ## 18 **POLITICAL ECONOMY**
**Lines:** 179-188

Discusses how political economy and institutional lock-in have historically favored lumpy energy technologies (via public underwriting, risk collectivization, and demonstrations of commitment), while market liberalization, innovation, and digitalization are now strengthening support for granular technologies that diversify actors, de-risk investment, and open markets to new entrants.

## 19 **CONCLUSIONS**
**Lines:** 189-192

Concludes that granular technologies empirically deliver faster diffusion, lower investment risk, faster learning, and higher social returns, and recommends that governments, firms, investors, scientists, and modelers explicitly incorporate unit scale/granularity as a criterion when designing decarbonization strategies, R&D portfolios, and scenario analyses.
