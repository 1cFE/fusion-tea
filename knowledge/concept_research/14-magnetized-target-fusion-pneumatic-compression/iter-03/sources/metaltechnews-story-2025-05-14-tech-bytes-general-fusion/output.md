The Elements of Innovation Discovered

Metal Tech News - May 14, 2025

Following its [initial plasma milestone](https://www.metaltechnews.com/story/2025/03/12/tech-bytes/general-fusion-achieves-plasma-milestone/2178.html) earlier this year, General Fusion Inc. successfully compressed magnetized plasma with a liquid lithium liner inside its Lawson Machine 26 fusion reactor – a critical advance toward demonstrating the viability of its magnetized target fusion technology.

As the dream of nuclear fusion appears to grow closer each day, General Fusion continues to advance a technology it has spent more than two decades developing – one that deviates from conventional designs by replacing massive magnetic fields or lasers with a liquid metal liner used to compress magnetized plasma.

At the core of every fusion system is plasma – a superheated state of matter where atoms are stripped of their electrons, allowing atomic nuclei to collide. If those collisions happen with enough force, the nuclei can fuse, releasing energy in the process.

It is the same reaction that powers the sun, recreated under controlled conditions here on Earth and widely regarded as one of the most difficult steps in developing a working fusion power system: creating, containing, and compressing the plasma in which it occurs.

To address that hurdle, General Fusion has been developing a system that replaces complex magnetic or laser confinement methods with a mechanically driven pulse designed to rapidly compress plasma inside a liquid metal chamber.

This approach, known as magnetized target fusion, forms the basis of the company's Lawson Machine 26 (LM26) prototype and is intended to demonstrate that fusion conditions can be achieved using simpler, more scalable technologies.

On April 29, General Fusion conducted the first integrated test of LM26, successfully compressing magnetized plasma with a liquid lithium liner inside the machine's full-scale chamber.

According to the company, early diagnostics showed increases in ion temperature and density, along with evidence that the lithium liner effectively trapped the plasma's magnetic field – an indication that the core mechanics of the system operated as designed.

In simpler terms, the plasma became hotter and more concentrated – conditions necessary for fusion – and the liquid metal liner appeared to hold the magnetic field in place during compression, a critical step in keeping the superheated material stable long enough for a reaction to occur.

This marked the first time the full LM26 system was operated as an integrated unit, combining plasma generation, compression hardware, and diagnostic instruments in a single run – something the company considers a foundational step in demonstrating that its fusion reactor can be scaled toward a working energy system.

"We successfully compressed a large-scale magnetized plasma with lithium using our world-first LM26 fusion demonstration machine," said General Fusion CEO Greg Twinney. "The full, integrated system and diagnostics operated safely and as designed."

With compression now demonstrated, the company plans to continue refining the system's performance, focusing on plasma stability, repeatability, and energy gain as it advances toward the conditions required for sustained fusion reactions.
