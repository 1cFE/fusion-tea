Zap generates fusion plasmas inside of two systems: FuZE (shown here) and FuZE-Q.

Members of the R&D team prepare for FuZE-Q operations.

A plasma gives off light from inside of the FuZE-Q chamber. Zap R&D primarily uses an isotope of hydrogen called deuterium as fuel, which glows pink or blue when it emits visible light.

First-of-their-kind banks of capacitors store up and release the pulses of electrical power that drive Z-pinch fusion.

Plasmas inside FuZE-Q are measured with a world-class suite of scientific instrumentation and diagnostics.

Zap’s plasma devices are compact and allow for rapid iteration and changes. Here a team of engineers reconfigures FuZE for a new series of tests.

A schematic of the Fusion Z-pinch Experiment device concept where a plasma is compressed to fusion conditions through a Z pinch and stabilized with plasma flow.

R&D campaigns are informed by theory and computational simulations. Test data and results feed back into these models to improve the fidelity of the simulations.

A simplified Z-pinch device is used as a plasma load for engineering advanced repetitive power supplies.

Sheared-flow-stabilized Z-pinch fusion doesn’t require magnets, cryogenics or high-powered lasers. The difference leads to a modular, flexible design with more compelling economics than other credible fusion concepts.
