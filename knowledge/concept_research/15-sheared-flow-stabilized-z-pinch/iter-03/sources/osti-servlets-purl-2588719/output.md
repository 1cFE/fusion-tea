I. INTRODUCTION

usion energy is a high consequence industry with the
potential to create tens of thousands of new jobs, help
address the United Nation Millennium Development
goals on climate change and impact the roughly 10
trillion-dollar (annual) energy industry. Over the past nearly 70
years of fusion research, a wide variety of reactor approaches
have been tested with varying levels of success. This diversity
evolved because researchers were still working to understand
the basic principles of plasma behavior, confinement
mechanisms, heating techniques, and other principles. This is
common in early-stage technologies; for example, the early
automotive industry pursued three competing technologies to
power the car: steam, internal combustion engine, and electric
motors [6],[7],[8],[9],[10]. This was also true of the aerospace
and fission industries, which pursued many designs before
downselecting to a few technologies that came to dominate their
respective industries.

Today, the fusion industry has yet to downselect to one, or
even several, dominant reactor designs. This industry has raised
more than 6 billion dollars in private investment with dozens of
startups putting forth a panoply of reactor approaches. These
include (but are not limited to) Inertial Confinement Fusion,
(ICF), Magnetic Confinement Fusion (MCF) Field Reversed
Configurations, Magnetic Mirrors, Tokamaks, Spherical
Tokamaks, Pinches, and Stellarators, among others

[1],[2],[3],[4],[5],[11]. All these reactor approaches have roots
in decades of public sector research at both the national
laboratories and at universities. In most cases, there are multiple
variations on the same basic approach that have evolved over
time. For example, the ICF community has worked on direct
drive, indirect drive, fast ignition, and Magnetized Liner Inertial
Fusion (MagLIF). In some cases, startups have proposed
combining aspects of two different fusion fields, like General
Fusion which has tested a reactor design that combines (1)
spinning up a plasma structure while (2) mechanically

Many of these fusion companies are “first-mover” firms that
face the universal challenges of building a new workforce,
educating investors and government backers, building new
regulatory frameworks, and developing new business models

[13]. Given the complexity and high risks of these challenges,
it is likely that many of these fusion firms will fail; but failure
will seed the market with trained staff, innovations, and
business models that successful firms will be able to leverage.
Moreover, with so many approaches being tested, the odds that
one or several firms achieve net power are improved. Generally,
these companies start by creating development roadmaps
involving a series of technical milestones – proof-of-concept
demonstrations – each with an associated cost, timeline, and
project schedule. Roadmaps are a common tool both inside
industry and government, to tackle early-stage disruptive
technology maturation and commercialization [14]. For these
roadmaps, investors often ask the companies to address the
highest technology risks first. For example, an Inertial Fusion
Energy company may be asked to demonstrate high-risk
technology like plasma optics, high-damage-threshold glass,
and an advanced laser diode - before receiving follow-on
funding to prototype the complete beamline. Disruptive,
technology-based, roadmaps can accelerate the average 10 year
to 15 plus year timeline needed to mature a disruptive
technology and prepare it for market [6],[7],[15].

Advancing fusion will require advancing the state of pulsed
power technology because many concepts have such high
energy requirements. The pulsed power pre-roadmap outlined
here was formed from meetings between professionals from the
so-called “Triple Helix” (Industry, Government, and
Academia) [16]. This pre-roadmap draws on input from all
three communities. It does not attempt to prescribe a dominant
reactor concept or a particular technology, but rather we focus
on components potentially common to all the approaches.
Ultimately, several fusion reactor designs may reach
commercial viability, as was true of both the fission and
aerospace industry. This review will focus on component
development where we will discuss some of the component
developments and maturation that are needed to produce
components to scale to make fusion power a reality.

The remainder of this review article is organized as follows.
Section II reviews the various approaches to fusion energy. This
sets the stage for the need for pulsed power components.
Section III presents the outcomes of the workshop on pulsed
power for fusion that was held at the Pulsed Power Conference
in June 2023. Section IV summarizes the key findings of this
review article.

II. APPROACHES TO FUSION ENERGY

The dominant fuel cycle for fusion power plants is the
deuterium-tritium (D-T) reaction [17]. In this approach, a
deuterium atom fuses with a tritium atom producing a 14 MeV
neutron [17]. To produce electricity, the neutron is captured by
a molten salt blanket, subsequently heating the molten salt [18].
A heat exchanger is then used to produce steam which turns
turbines. Underpinning this D-T reaction is a high neutron flux,
which may require radiation-hardened components.

There are also aneutronic reaction fuel cycles that instead use
a helium-deuterium (He [3] -D) reaction where the neutrons
produced have 2.45 MeV energy rather than the 14 MeV energy
of the D-T fuel cycle [17]. Radiation and neutron damage are
reduced with an aneutronic approach, but higher
Bremsstrahlung flux is present in many of the He [3] fuel cycles.
Thus, both approaches may require radiation hardened
components.

The approaches to fusion energy applications include two
dominant approaches with a multitude of hybrid topologies, all
of which are being pursued nationally and internationally by the
Triple Helix (Figure 1). The first major grouping is the MCF
devices. These include Tokamaks, Stellarators, Reversed-Field
Pinches, and other devices which confine the plasma by the
Lorentz force using magnetic fields [8]. These magnetically
based fusion approaches may have a continuous confinement
mode, but most operate in a long-pulsed mode on the order of a
millisecond or longer. In addition to the solenoidal, toroidal,
poloidal, and Stellarator field coils, the magnetic field
confinement approach may include magnetic mirrors [3],[19].
Subsequently, if cryogenic magnets are utilized for the
confinement, limited or little pulsed power may be required for
the magnetic fusion power plant, but may require fast pulsed
power switches to shut down fault modes that can result in first

III. OUTCOMES OF THE WORKSHOP ON PULSED POWER FOR
FUSION

The common technologies identified for all the pulsed powerdriven fusion approaches include high voltage switching and
energy storage, as well as modular pulsed power topologies and
additive manufacturing. A number of these value chain
components/elements were discussed at the workshop. The goal
of the workshop was to identify technologies that could be
utilized for near-term to far-term fusion applications in the 5
year to 15 year time frame, and various speakers outlined
development paths realizing operational fusion generators by
the early 2030s. The workshop focused on component and
energy storage needs for pulsed power drivers that can supply
currents of 0.5 MA to 90 MA with pulse widths ranging from
50 ns to 10 µs at voltages from 100 kV to 10 MV. Repetitive
operation of components in the 0.1 pps to 100 pps rate are of

interest for a fusion power plant. This corresponds to average
powers from about 5 MW to 100 MW. The requisite lifetimes
are from 1 million pulses to over a billion pulses before
maintenance is required. These requirements encompass laser
driven fusion applications such as Z-pinches, and Z Machinelike MagLIF fusion target requirements as well as many other
approaches. Other requirements are of interest as well,
including repetitive pulsed power drivers for electromagnets as
well as crowbar switches for fault mode protection. These
would also be applicable to MCF approaches.

Workshop speakers included scientists and engineers from all
branches of the triple helix, and multiple pulsed power
approaches for fusion applications were covered. For example,
SNL presented an approach for the scaleup of the Z Machine
for the Next Generation of Pulsed Power (NGPP). LLNL
presented the architecture of the National Ignition Facility
(NIF) as well as the reliability issues related to operation of a
large facility. First Light Fusion presented their scale-up of a
NGPP type driver for acceleration of projectiles for fusion
applications. Zap Energy presented their path forward for a
pulsed power driven, flow stabilized Z pinch fusion source
which will utilize solid state pulsed power drivers.

In addition to the presentations on fusion approaches, General
Atomics discussed the challenges for building high voltage
capacitors as well as the issues associated with scaling-up of the
production process for large facilities. Peak Nano, a materials
company specializing in polymers, presented advanced
dielectric films for high voltage capacitors with higher energy
density and operating temperature. The energy storage needs of
a fusion system are not relegated to only capacitors; there are
various dielectrics and pulse compression stages that may be
required by pulsed power-based fusion devices.

The common themes discussed at the workshop were energy
storage, high voltage switching, and modular pulsed power
architectures. As an example, the fusion approach taken by Zap
Energy utilizes high voltage capacitor banks to drive a Z pinch
load that implodes in many microseconds, whereas the
approaches used by First Light Fusion and SNL utilize Marx
banks that are discharged and compressed to deliver energy to
a load in 100 ns to 300 ns. The Marx generators used by First
Light and SNL operate in the 5 MV to 10 MV range, while the
Zap Energy approach operates at voltages of 50 kV to 200 kV.
The Marx generator then pulse charges additional compression
stages, ultimately compressing a microsecond pulse down to a
hundred or few hundred nanosecond pulse. Concurrently, the
switches for each Marx stage traditionally have operated at 200
kV and 100 kA, and under fault mode conditions up to 200 kA.
Advanced dielectrics that can operate at 100s of kV,
repetitively, with a long lifetime, also require development. The
Marx switches require a 5 ns to 10 ns risetime, whereas the Zap
Energy approach may require a switch with a slower risetime
where the capacitors are discharged in 100s of nanoseconds up
to several microseconds.

The laser-based approach to fusion which utilizes pulsed
power has different requirements than MDD approaches. One
example is NIF, where a glass laser driven hohlraum approach
to laser inertial fusion, utilizes capacitor banks to pump very
large flashlamps. The flashlamps are resonantly tuned to the
laser glass amplifiers which are used to ablate and ultimately
implode a D-T filled target. The flashlamp currents and pulse

_A. Component Value Chain Elements for Fusion Energy_
Some of the key technologies that presently limit the
advancement of fusion power are reliable, long lifetime, pulsed
power components (energy storage, switching), additive
manufacturing, and potentially modular architectures. By
improving these value chain elements and their supply,
commercial fusion energy may be realized sooner.

The fusion technology value chain required to meet the
expectations of a commercial fusion power plant or even
prototypes include a disruptive change in switching and energy
storage components. The present demand for high voltage
components and laboratory systems are marginally met by
existing suppliers or by internal design efforts; neither of which
display the product production capability required for a long
lifetime power plant. Given that the power plants require
lifetimes of millions to billions of shots under continuous
operation, new revolutionary switching technologies and
energy storage components with the necessary current and
voltage specifications require strategic development and
funding. In fact, interest in meeting such needs is a very recent
development. A good example of that is the case of pulsed
power where the focus is on developing realistic near-term (1
year to 5 years), mid-term (5 years to 10 years) and long-term
(10 years to 20 years) supply and value chain progression for
1 [st] through 3 [rd] generation fusion power development needs.
However, all these efforts are hampered by supply and value
chains as well as research funding that does not meet the needs
of a disruptive, high consequence industry.

_B. Energy Storage_
The high voltage energy storage in a pulsed power system
derives its prime power from the wall plug. The stored energy

is then discharged in a short time scale, increasing the peak
power delivered to a target which can generate a fusion yield.
The number of compression stages required is dictated by the
desired current risetime into the load and the output of the

**required for fusion energy applications.**

The two primary means to store energy in fusion applications
are capacitive or inductive. The capacitive approach is the most
widely utilized energy storage in pulsed power systems and
utilizes closing switches. An inductive energy storage approach
can have 10 times the energy density of the capacitive approach,
but must have available efficient, fast risetime opening
switches. Currently, opening switches absorb 50% to 60% of
the energy passing through them, which results in the inductive
energy storage approach having poor efficiency [37].

High voltage capacitors are typically charged to 25 kV to 100
kV. The capacitors can be discharged at currents on the order
of 100 kA to 200 kA, although the capacitors should be derated
to improve the lifetime under repetitive operation. The 100 kV,
2.6 µF high energy capacitors utilized in the Z Marx bank weigh
~100 kg. and are built in a metallic housing [38]. They have an
energy density of 0.26 J/g and utilize paper as the dielectric

[39]. The lifetime of these capacitors varies from 10 [4] shots to
10 [5] shots, which is dependent on the charge voltage and voltage
reversal [38],[39]. Higher energy density capacitors are
available with published energy densities of 3 J/g [40], but these
may have limited lifetimes and would need to be substantially
derated for the billion shot lifetime required for fusion
prototypes. Additionally, these capacitors discharge in the
millisecond regime and are limited to voltages below 25 kV.

Smaller capacitors which are used in LTDs, fast Marx banks,
or IMGs are housed in a plastic case and typically have
capacitances of 40 nF to 60 nF [41]. All these capacitors can be
fabricated using metalized dielectric films which increases the
energy density but require a trade off in the peak current rating
and lifetime. An encouraging experiment by General Atomics
showed that the lifetime increased by at least seven times with
no failures when operated at cryogenic temperatures [41].

The state-of-the-art capacitors for microsecond to 100s of
microsecond discharge times are typically metalized film and
have an energy density of 1 J/cm [3] to 3 J/cm [3] [41]. This energy

leveraging these materials for long-lifetime applications at
lower fields, but the linear permittivity is retained. As such,
additional validation tests and lifetime measurements need to
be demonstrated at a large scale and volume to develop the
relevant statistics and reliability data. Based on historical
trends, if a new material were to be developed today, the
average time to scaleup manufacturing if not presently in
progress is 10 years to 15 years [44]. This period between
research and small-scale demonstration and commercialization
is the valley of death [45]. One option to accelerate this
transition of technology from the laboratory is public private
partnerships [46],[47].

Additional material development as well as automated
assembly processes are required to further increase the lifetime
and energy density of the large, high voltage energy density
capacitors. Improvements of 2 to 6 orders of magnitude in
lifetime are required for fusion energy applications. In parallel,
robotics and additive manufacturing are required to increase
production capability and to decrease the labor costs of
fabrication. To put this issue in perspective, one power plant
may require 10,000 capacitors to 216,000 capacitors. The
delivery times for this order of magnitude volume is on the
order of 4 years to 6 years. We should note that labor is a major
fraction of the capacitor cost at the present time. Workforce
training may be required at the university level with on-the-job
experience. Machine learning coupled with robotics could
substantially reduce the work force numbers required for
fabrication of the capacitors but not eliminate it.

As it stands, if 150 fusion power plants were to be built today
to service the United States, the time required to build the
required capacitors is approximately 125 years to 250 years
given the western world’s available manufacturers and supply
chain of high voltage capacitors. This assumes that the
dielectrics and capacitor high voltage feedthroughs as well as
housings are available commercially. One can argue that
commercial companies will expand to meet the supply chain
requirements, however there is a substantial lead time to
scaleup the equipment required for production.

All the high voltage, high energy capacitor-based energy
storage systems today utilize dielectric films or paper in the
capacitors. There are a number of other capacitor dielectrics,
including ceramics, ceramic composites, and semiconductorbased dielectrics [48],[49],[50]. Unfortunately, many of these

capacitor dielectrics have energy densities well below that of
dielectric film capacitors and have limited production volumes.

The capacitor dielectrics based on wideband gap (WBG)
semiconductors have an interesting potential to serve as a
dielectric since they can hold off electric fields approximately
30 to 40 times that of silicon.

Double layer capacitors have energy densities an order of
magnitude higher than any other available high voltage
capacitors, but can operate only up to 2.5 V to 3.5 V.
Additionally, double layer capacitors have slow discharge
times, typically on the order of milliseconds due to the diffusion
times through the double layer [51]. Thus, they are not
applicable for the rapid discharge rates or high voltage
applications in the pulsed power required for fusion
applications.

Based on an in-depth review of available capacitor
technologies that are applicable to the fusion industry,
substantial investment in new dielectrics that can store orders
of magnitude higher energy and can be assembled into large
volume capacitors is a prerequisite for the fusion energy
applications. Significant advances in capacatior technologies
have been achieved using traditional bialially oriented
polypropylene (BOPP) films, however additional work is being
met with diminishing returns and new films will need to be
developed to meet the energy density needs for fusion energy

[52]. This could be achieveved by increasing the technical
readiness of one or more of the existing films, or potentially by
developing a machine learning-driven materials discovery.

Concurrently, self-assembling dielectric and dielectric foil
manufacturing techniques are of interest; however, substantial
resources are required to develop and mature these
technologies. In parallel, to meet the near-term and mid-term
fusion prototype requirements, advanced robotic assembly,
additive manufacturing which can print cases and high current
contacts, as well as machine learning needs to be matured with
both private investment and government investments. The
humanoid robots being developed by companies in the US and
PRC could substantially accelerate scaleup and production of
high voltage capacitors. However, these applications are in their
infancy and incorporation of these technologies will not likely
be motivated by the conventional commercial market-base,
further necessitating the need for public-private partnerships
and other funding sources.

Although we have presented the case for advanced capacitor
dielectric development and robotics, along with additive
manufacturing, we should briefly discuss alternative energy
storage approaches. As mentioned, Inductive energy storage
has approximately 10 times the energy density of capacitive
energy storage [36]. Since inductive energy storage stores the
energy in a magnetic field, one must establish a current in an
inductor and then open a switch to stop the current. The energy
stored in the inductor is then transferred into a load or an
intermediate compression stage [36]. This technology was
demonstrated at NRL using exploding wire fuses [36].
However, this is not practical for repetitive fusion energy
applications unless an efficient opening switch is developed,
one that is based on solid state switches and can be operated
repetitively and not absorb 45% to 50% of the energy while
opening.

The inductive energy storage approach may also benefit from
hybrid battery capacitor technologies [53]. It is outside the
scope of this pre-roadmap to determine the scaleup of this
technology for fusion applications. Since these are at the
present time based on supercapacitors which operate at 2.5 V to
3.5 V, we cannot foresee a practical way to scaleup the
technology at this time.

We would also suggest that practical high risk, high payoff
disruptive technologies be identified and receive investment.
Potentially, there may be disruptive technologies that should be
matured for the fusion energy field. These are outside of the
present pre-roadmap but may be of interest to both private

repetitive switches for fusion power plants.

_C. Advanced Switching for Fusion Applications_
For laboratory prototyping in the near-term, the spark gap
switches used in Marx generators meet the experimental shot
rate needs of once a day and could be increased to 10 times to
20 times per day. Spark gaps which consist of two metal
electrodes and insulated by a gas or a liquid have not changed
substantially in over 50 years [36],[37],[53]. These spark gaps
typically operate from a few 10s of kV up to 10 MV and have
risetimes on the order of 5 ns to 10 ns in Marx bank operation.
The Z Marx bank consists of spark gap switched stages, each
comprised of one to two capacitors and a switch. The Marx
bank architectures have traditionally been operated from 1 MV
up to 10 MV [54],[55]. The Z Machine at SNL utilizes thirty
six, 6.5 MV Marx banks that are charged in parallel and
discharged in series [54],[55]. The lifetime of similar Marx
spark gaps are 10 [3] shots to 10 [6] shots and can be operated in
either single shot or repetitive mode. The lifetime is a function
of the current (action) or charge transfer, as well as the pulse
width. The typical peak current is on the order of 100 kA to 200
kA. Low service lifetime, and cost of ownership challenges
need to be addressed. Spark gaps suffer from limited lifetime,

and thus, greater required maintenance. Spark gaps with
electrodes that can be self-adjusting, with lower erosion and
subsequently longer lifetime may be a possibility. Additionally
spark gaps with lower erosion, SF6 free and lower maintenance
warrant investigation for repetitive operation.

The short lifetime of the switches and timing jitter is an issue
for many of the pulsed power architectures and fusion
architectures being developed in industry, especially as the
spark gap surface ages and erodes [53],[55]. Thus, we believe
that solid state switching development may be required to meet
the operational, long lifetime, low jitter, and trigger
requirements for repetitive fusion applications. Small scale,
IMG and LTD modules which utilize solid state switching have
been developed but may not be scalable to the voltage and
current levels required for a fusion power plant. These scaled
systems do not yet approach the energy stored levels required
for fusion applications, for the solid-state switches with a 5 ns
to 10 ns risetime, voltage holdoff and current carrying
capability are not yet available.

Up until a few years ago, silicon was the dominant
semiconductor used in power semiconductor switches. A 48
kV, 1 MA switch was developed by Excelitas (formerly Silicon
Power) that had a risetime of 10s of nanoseconds. Additionally,
silicon thyristors and gate turn off thyristors with risetimes of
several microseconds could switch 10 kA to 1 MA repetitively,
based on the pulse width and peak current [57]. However, for
the fusion applications, the existing holdoff voltage, peak
current, di/dt risetimes are insufficient for the pulsed power
architectures identified. Faster switches, such as MOSFETs and
bipolar transistors could switch 6.5 kV to 10 kV with 10s of
nanosecond risetimes albeit, predominantly the 10 kV modules
produced commercially have not been optimized for the fast
risetimes required for pulsed power applications [58]. The
current may however be limited to around 1 kA (or less) for an
individual device. Higher current may be possible depending on
the size of the die. At the substrate level we may be able to
parallel or series the devices or increase the area of each die for
the high current and voltages required for pulsed power drivers.
This warrants engineering development for pulsed power
applications.

Recent wideband gap (WBG) materials have appeared on the
market with 4H-SiC being the dominant material used in
commercial MOSFETs. Assuming little to no defects, the 4HSiC bulk material can hold off 2200 kV/cm. As such, 6.5 kV
to10 kV MOSFETs (Wolfspeed, Genesic) are available on the
commercial market that can switch 500 A or more depending
on the pulse width. Custom MOSFETs have been produced at
15 kV to 20 kV hold off voltages [59]. This progress is
encouraging but still does not meet the current specifications
required nor the desire for a modestly priced, large production
scale, 100 kV to 200 kV, 100 kA to 200 kA switch, with
nanosecond risetimes.

Other emerging wideband gap materials include GaN (Eb=2.0
MV/cm), AlGaN (Eb=8.1 MV/cm), AlN (Eb=1.4 MV/cm) and
diamond (Eb=10.0 MV/cm) [59],[60],[61]. Thus, a 200 kV
switch would be 0.02 cm to 0.11 cm thick, depending on the
material. Approximately 1 cm [2] to 2 cm [2] of material would be
required to conduct 50 kA/cm [2] to 150 kA/cm [2] depending on the
desired current density and the on-resistance required, in
addition to the repetition rate required for the specific fusion

![](images/tmpo1gbsrwb.pdf-8-0.png)

application. Advanced packaging with advanced cooling
technology is required, as well as large area defect free
substrates or the ability to grow “large” scale epitaxial layers.
This is well within the state-of-the-art in semiconductor growth
technology. However, no large volume, commercial market
exists today; thus, no development has been done to scaleup the
WBG wafers or devices to this size. We believe that there is a
gap in technology development in scaling from the existing
WBG substrate level up to the sizes required for the 100 kV to
200 kV, high current switches required for fusion applications.
Exploratory funding is required to understand the electron
transport and breakdown in large area, WBG substrates. This
includes ballistic or high energy electron transport in
semiconductors. As well as mitigation of defects in large area
devices.

If higher voltage or higher current switches are acquired, the
100 kV to 200 kV switch building blocks could be packaged or
mounted in series scaling to high voltage switches or multi
megampere switches with 5 ns to 10 ns risetimes. New
packaging with active cooling may be required for these
specific applications. This will require further development.

Researchers at Imperial College have achieved kiloampere
operation at repetitive rates in excess of 100 pps using series
connected Solidtrons from Excelitas [63], [64]. The risetime of
the series-parallel switch is less than 5 ns [63], [64]. This is
intriguing since commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) siliconbased devices were triggered in parallel and in a series topology
using fast ionization physics [62],[63]. If Solidtrons were to be
built using WBG materials, it is conceivable that a 100 kV to
200 kV, substrate level switch, could be rapidly developed.

The silicon opening switch and Drift Step Recovery Diode
(DSRD) opening switch are two candidates if inductive energy
storage were to be used [65]; however, as we have previously
mentioned, the opening switches typically absorb ~50% of the
energy and would require substantial active cooling. The use of
opening switches could potentially reduce the overall gain of a
fusion power plant by 50% or more.

Magnetic switching is a technology that was pioneered for
radar transmitters for WWII in the 1940s. Innovations in
magnetic switching at LLNL, SNL and at Cymer in the 19801990 timeframe suggest that this technology should be explored
further [65],[66]. Examples of this technology, include the Mag
1D pulsers at LLNL and the repetitive high energy pulsed
power (RHEPP) module at SNL [65],[66]. Additionally, new
materials such as Finemet and new generation of MetGlas have
reduced the losses in the magnetic materials and increased the
efficiency substantially of the magnetically switched pulsed
power topologies [68]. When the magnetic pulse technology is
combined with solid state switching topologies many of the
slower risetime, pulsed power-driven fusion topologies may be
achievable today.

Machine learning may help optimize manufacturing methods
and yield for semiconductor-based switches. Many of the
technologies mentioned may initially have low yields, which is
common for new semiconductor fabrication process. Given the
volumes required for the semiconductor switches intended for
fusion applications, characterization and packaging of the
switches will require automated production facilities to reduce
the cost of the technology, including robotic assembly.

Disruptive technologies should also be explored that may
utilize emerging materials, some of which could potentially be
used in a self-avalanche mode or be utilized in a photoavalanche or photoconductive solid state (PCSS) mode, which
substantially reduces the trigger requirements [68],[69]. The
new WBG substrates integrated with the new large array laser
diode modules could potentially result in 100 kV to 200 kV
switches at multi-kiloampere current capability. Ultimately
these switches could have risetimes on the order of 5 ns to 10
ns.

_D. Pulsed Power Topologies_
The approach to drive a fusion reaction will dictate the
necessary pulsed power circuit topology. Ultimately, high
voltage and high current switches will be required for the pulsed
power driver, and these should be standardized across multiple
approaches. If these approaches cannot be selected _a priori_,
then pulsed power components or modular sub-systems such as
LTDs or IMGs that can be utilized in generalized series or
parallel combinations will need to be developed. By
standandardizing these modular sub-systems, the development
of fusion reactors would be accelerated and the products would
be flexible enough to be adapted to future needs. Additionally,
the operation of a fusion reactor could be streamlined by
swapping out sub-systems for maintance.

IV. SUMMARY

We have discussed pulsed power driver circuit topologies and
development approaches that are required for a low
maintenance fusion power plant. The question remains, how

![](images/tmpo1gbsrwb.pdf-9-0.png)

does one develop these technologies? Given the reality that the
fusion companies are focused on utilizing all available
resources to demonstrate a near-to-mid-term a fusion load that
can generate an efficient yield, the impetus to develop
components that can fulfill supply chain requirements for a
fusion power plant is approaching and looms as a significant
scale-up gap. Concurrently, the depth of material science,
pulsed power science and engineering, semiconductor
fabrication, automation including robotics, and machine
learning require an integrated approach that to date has not been
demonstrated for rapid component development. As such we
would propose a public private partnership or consortia that can
openly develop these complex supply chain technologies. Once
the technology is jointly developed then commercial companies
could manufacture the technology.

This consortia approach was explored at the IEEE workshop
on Pulsed Power for Fusion. Academia, commercial
companies, and national laboratories enthusiastically endorsed
forming the consortia, and then rapidly transitioning the
technologies, as well as the engineering and science into the
commercial sector. The logistics need to be discussed at further
length, however the opportunity to advance the pulsed power
component supply chain into the 21 [st] century and concurrently
meet the needs of the fusion community is unprecedented.

The ability to develop the technology in addition to an
integrated simulation and modeling effort will require test
facilities to validate the technologies, determine operational
characteristics and evaluate component lifetime. Once the
technologies are validated, scaleup prototyping will be required
and the components tested in a subscale facility representative
of a module that will be operated in a fusion power plant.
Specifically, a demonstration module or modules representative
of a rep-rated fusion power plant.
