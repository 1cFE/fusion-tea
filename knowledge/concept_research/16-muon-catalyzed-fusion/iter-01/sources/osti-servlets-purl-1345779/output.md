## **ABSTRACT** 

- 18 The Spallation Neutron Source (SNS) has acquired extensive operational experience of a pulsed proton 19 superconducting linear accelerator (SCL) as a user facility. Numerous lessons have been learned in its 20 first 10 years operation to achieve a stable and reliable operation of the SCL. In this paper, an overview of 

- 21 the SNS SCL design, qualification of superconducting radio frequency (SRF) cavities and ancillary 

- 22 subsystems, an overview of the SNS cryogenic system, the SCL operation including SCL output energy 

- 23 history and downtime statistics, performance stability of the SRF cavities, efforts for SRF cavity 

- 24 performance recovery and improvement at the SNS, and maintenance activities for cryomodules are 25 introduced. 

- 27 Keywords: superconducting linear accelerator; superconducting radio-frequency cavity; pulsed operation; 28 spallation neutron source. 

## **1. Introduction** 

- 33 The Spallation Neutron Source (SNS) provides the most intense pulsed neutron beams in the world for 34 scientific research and industrial development. The neutrons are produced by the spallation process using 35 an accelerator-based system that delivers short proton pulses to a target and neutron moderator systems 36 [1]. Figure 1 shows the layout of the SNS accelerator system. The front end (FE) system produces the 37 negative hydrogen ion beam required for the injection into the accumulator ring downstream. In the FE, 38 the ion beam is also chopped for the clean extraction from the ring to the target. The beam from the FE is 39 injected into the linear accelerator (Linac) that consists of conventional normal conducting (NC) radio 40 frequency (RF) structures and superconducting RF (SRF) structures. For injection into the accumulator 41 ring, the negatively charged ions are stripped of their two electrons using a diamond foil. The ring 

- 42 accumulates successive bunches from the Linac and compresses a 1-ms long beam pulse into a 700-ns 43 pulse. Such intense pulses are sent into the target for neutron production at 60 pulses per second (pps), 5.2 44 million times a day. The time average beam power on the target is 1.4 MW. The SNS accelerator complex 45 was commissioned in 2006 and tremendous efforts have been made for reliable operation at the design 46 beam power. Since 2011, the SNS accelerator system has been providing neutron production with 47 availability 90% – 92 %.

- 53 Since the design beam power at the target was almost an order of magnitude higher than the then existing 54 pulsed accelerator-based neutron facilities, all subsystems of the SNS were designed and developed for 55 substantial improvements compared to existing accelerators and some subsystems are first of a kind. 56 Many performance and reliability aspects were unknown and unpredictable, and it took time to 57 understand the systems as a whole and to determine the need for additional performance improvements. 58 From the series of tests and operational experience, more understanding of systems and their limiting 59 conditions in the pulsed mode operation are being obtained at high duty factor operation. 

- 60 The main beam parameters for the accelerator, such as beam energy, beam pulse length at the target and 61 beam repetition rate were determined from the needs of the neutron scattering science. To best achieve 62 these parameters, accelerator design studies were conducted and the option of a full-energy Linac and 63 accumulator ring was chosen [1-4]. The studies estimated that there was a minor difference in cost, 64 however the full energy injection would be a simpler option for a single purpose machine and for a beam 65 power upgrade to multi-megawatts in the future. For the Linac technology, a combination of a NC Linac 66 and a Superconducting Linac (SCL) was adopted [5]. The FE system consists of a negative hydrogen ion 67 source, a radio frequency quadrupole and a medium energy beam transport line for matching the beam 68 into the Linac. The Linac consists of six drift tube Linac (DTL) structures, four coupled cavity Linac 69 (CCL) modules and twenty three cryomodules in the SCL. The output energies at each structure are 70 described in figure 1. 

- 72 The SNS SCL is the first large-scale high energy superconducting proton Linac that provides high beam 73 power using negative charged hydrogen beams. The use of superconducting elliptical shape cavities for 74 the acceleration of non-relativistic particles makes this accelerator a very important milestone. Another 75 important aspect of the superconducting Linac is the fact that it is operated under pulsed conditions. The 76 SRF cavities in the SNS SCL have the characteristic of being powered in a pulse mode, 1-ms beam pulses 77 at 60 pps, which is unique among superconducting accelerators for ions in routine operation. The SCL 78 utilizes two SRF cavity geometries; medium beta cavity with a geometric beta of 0.61 and high beta 79 cavity with that of 0.81 to cover the energy range in the SCL. 

- 82 **2. SNS Superconducting Linac design** 

- 84 After preliminary conceptual design studies for a SCL option were conducted in 1999 [5], the Linac 85 baseline above 186 MeV was changed from NC CCL structures to SRF structures in 2000. Justifications 86 for the SCL option for the SNS were; 1) higher real estate accelerating gradient leads to shorter Linac and 87 klystron gallery lengths, thus cheaper construction cost, 2) Cheaper operating cost since SCL requires less 88 electricity, 3) individually powered SRF cavity provides high operational flexibility which leads to high 89 availability, and 4) the extremely low vacuum and larger beam acceptance in SCL results in lower beam 90 loss. 

- 92 Although the SNS SCL was the first application of SRF cavities for a pulsed high power ion accelerator, 93 the science and technology for critical components such as SRF cavities and fundamental power couplers 94 were well-developed and these served as the design basis for the SCL. Two constraints drove the high 95 level design for the SNS SRF cavities: 1) limit the peak power of the fundamental power couplers to a 96 maximum of 350 kW in the very early stage of the SNS SCL design, later increased to 550 kW and 2) 97 limit the cavity peak surface electric field to 27.5 MV/m due to concerns related with field emission, later 98 increased to 35 MV/m for the high beta cavities [6] by adopting electro-polishing as a surface processing 99 technique. In reality, electro-polishing was only applied to several high beta cavities because the 

- 100 technology for production cavities was not sufficiently mature at the time of cavity production. The 550101 kW RF power limit per SRF cavity corresponded to klystrons that were available commercially and did 102 not require new development. 

103 104 After investigating a variety of SCL configurations, a rough cost optimum was found using only two 105 cavity geometries: medium beta cavity and high beta cavity.  The cavity shapes were designed to optimize 106 the transit-time factor, balance the peak surface magnetic and electric fields, increase the resonant 107 mechanical frequencies, minimize the effects of Lorentz detuning, and take into account possible issues 108 with higher-order cavity modes and available RF power [7-9].  The design accelerating gradients were 109 chosen at 10.1 MV/m and 15.8 MV/m for the medium beta cavity and the high beta cavity respectively. 110 The cavity unloaded quality factor was set to accommodate the possibility of operating under heavy field 111 emission regime. A coaxial type fundamental power coupler (FPC) with a single window was chosen 112 based on the 508 MHz FPC experiences at KEK [10]. The higher order mode (HOM) coupler geometry 113 was scaled from the Tesla Test Facility (TTF) HOM coupler. The mechanical tuner was scaled from the 114 TTF-Saclay tuner and later modified to incorporate a piezo actuator for dynamic detuning of the SRF 115 cavities in case the effect of the Lorentz force detuning would be too large to be controlled with the low 116 level RF system alone. The SNS cryomodule design was based on the CEBAF cryomodule with 117 improvements from lessons learned at LHC, TTF and CEBAF 12-GeV upgrade programs. The 118 cryomodules and all of the internal components were designed, processed, and assembled at Thomas 119 Jefferson National Accelerator Facility (TJNAF) [11].   Between cryomodules there are 1.6-m long warm 120 sections for magnets and diagnostics, and differential pumping stations were adopted at both ends of the 121 SCL for vacuum transition [12]. ## **3. Cryomodule and SRF cavity qualification** 

134 Qualifying the components as a fully integrated system, and identifying and understanding the limiting 135 conditions are critical to achieve a reliable operation of cryomodules. Figure 2 shows the cryomodules 136 installed in the SNS Linac tunnel. Several extensive performance qualifications of cryomodules and SRF 137 cavities at various operating temperatures and repetition rates were conducted with cryomodules installed 138 in the tunnel in conjunction with all supporting systems [13]. Interlocks and machine protections have 139 been improved based on results from the qualification studies and from the accumulated operational 140 experience. Some important issues for reliable SCL operation such as end-group thermal instability, 141 HOM coupler issues, cavity-coupler interactions, adsorbed gases on the cold surface of SRF cavities and 142 collective effects between cavities were identified and studied. The term ‘collective effects’ in this paper 143 refers to interactions of electrons between cavities in a cryomodule where electrons from one cavity can 144 affect other cavities’ performances. In particular thermal instability in the end-groups of SRF cavities due 145 to electron activity such as field emission and multipacting is the main limiting factor of the accelerating 146 gradients in operation at the SNS. Also as the repetition rate was increased, more severe collective effect 147 between cavities was observed. In the following, the limiting accelerating gradient refers to the maximum 148 accelerating gradient that can be achieved in a cavity with RF loading only for a short period of time, 149 typically less than 24 hours, while the stable accelerating gradient refers to the accelerating gradient that 150 can be maintained stably during production run with beam acceleration. The stable operating gradient is 151 found to be empirically about 85% of the limiting gradient at the SNS. 152 153 154 **Insert Figure 2** 155 156 157 _3.1_ 

## _3.1 Initial operation at 4.4K_ 

- 158 159 Since most sub-systems were not commissioned for the operation at the full duty factor in the early stage 160 of the SNS operation, the RF repetition rate was limited to 10 pps and stable accelerating gradients for all 161 the SRF cavities were initially established at 10 pps. In addition, the cryogenic plant’s sub-atmospheric 162 system was not available for the initial cavity and cryomodule tests in 2005 and the SCL system was 163 initially operated at 4.4 K instead of the design operating temperature of 2.1 K. At 4.4 K and 10 pps, most 164 SRF cavities in the SCL could be operated stably at their design accelerating gradients, and a good 165 portion of the cavities could even be operated at higher gradients. The SCL operation for neutron 166 production at 2.1 K started in 2007, once the sub-atmospheric part of the cryogenic plant became 167 operational and the repetition rate was increased to 30 pps and then 60 pps. Additional studies of the 168 maximum achievable gradients in the SRF cavities indicated that the limiting factors in the cavities were 169 not related to the operating temperature. The operating temperature of the SNS SRF cavities were 170 reviewed, and cavity parameters and their interplay were reevaluated, giving details on the possible range 171 of operating parameters which can be achieved at SNS in pulsed conditions [14]. 

- 173 The transient nature of pulsed operation allows a larger variation of temperatures within the niobium 174 surface, so that during the pulse the surface resistance of the material can change substantially. There are 

175 no BCS limitations at the SNS duty and pulse length up to the critical field when the helium temperature 176 is less than 4.5 K, which was also verified with individual cryomodule tests at 60 pps and both at 2.1 K 177 and 4.4 K. It is mainly due to the pulsed nature of the SNS operation and relatively low operating 178 frequency compared to frequencies for electron machines (e.g. 1.3 and 1.5 GHz). The SNS SCL operation 179 at 4.4 K was conducted only at 20-pps or lower repetition rate because the cryogenic load from all 180 cryomodules at a higher repetition rate exceeds the SNS cryogenic capacity designed for 2.1-K operation. 

- 183 _3.2 Stable operating gradients_ 

185 The main limiting factor for the stable operating gradients of the SNS SRF cavities is related to the 186 thermal load from electrons due to field emission and multipacting [15, 16]. In the SNS SCL, the 187 cryogenic load from electron activities is equal or slightly higher than the thermal loads from BCS 188 resistance at 2.1 K and residual resistance. Since the SNS cryogenic plant has an enough capacity for the 189 2.1-K operation as described in the section 4, there is no concern with the cavity quality factor as long as 190 its operation is stable. However, this could be a critical issue for continuous wave machines, if there is a 191 severe electron activity at an operating gradient. In the SNS SCL thermal instabilities caused by electron 192 activities is more of an issue than quality factors of cavities. The thermal instability occurs in the end 193 groups, leading to a partial quench or gas burst triggering a vacuum interlock. The thermal instability of 194 the end groups of the SNS SRF cavities is not dominated by material defects because the magnetic field is 195 low on those surfaces. In contrast, primary and secondary electrons emitted at various locations on 196 surfaces of the SRF cavities can get accelerated by the electric field and end up depositing enough power 197 on the end-group surfaces to generate a thermal instability especially because the end-groups are made of 198 the reactor grade niobium whose residual resistivity ratio (RRR) is typically 30 and are not directly cooled 199 by the helium bath. In order to increase the RRR of the SNS end groups during the cavity production, the 200 end-groups were heat treated but the RRR increased only to around 50. When the end group reaches its 201 instability threshold, an intermediate state or partially quenched state can develop, which is characteristic 202 of low field region thermal instability. The main cavity body region has the opposite thermal stability 203 characteristic, having a higher thermal stability against a thermal load from electron loadings than the end 204 groups but having a faster thermal runaway with localized heating from material defect or from heavy 205 multipacting. Most SRF cavities in the SCL show end-group instability evidenced by thermometry and 206 slow quench developments over many pulses. Only a few cavities show evidence of main cavity body 207 thermal instability. The radiation onsets were characterized in the SNS SCL after conditioning and were 208 measured at an average gradient of 10 MV/m with 3-MV/m standard deviation. 209 210 At a repetition rate of 15 pps, the average limiting gradients of the medium- and the high-beta cavities are 211 16.7 MV/m and 17.5 MV/m respectively, which exceed the design gradients. At this repetition rates the 212 individual limiting gradient that is measured by powering one cavity in a cryomodule at a time, are about 213 the same as collective limits that are measured with operating all the cavities in a cryomodule 214 simultaneously. Linac output energy of 1 GeV at 15 pps was first achieved in February 2007. The 215 operating gradients were set to about 85 % of the individual limiting gradients and 78 cavities out of 81 216 cavities were in service for this run. In contrast, 1-GeV operation has yet to be achieved at the full duty 217 factor and 60 pps. 

- 219 Clear evidence of collective effects limiting the operating gradients of the SRF cavities can be observed at 220 a repetition rate of 30 pps or higher. The severity of the collective effects are not only determined by the 221 amplitude of the accelerating gradients in the cavities of a cryomodule, but also by their relative RF 222 phases because both affect the trajectories of the electrons, energies of the electrons and the locations 223 where the electrons impact on the cavity surfaces. Due to the collective effects, cavities become thermally 224 unstable at lower gradients than the ones at lower repetition rate operation, thus limiting the final Linac 225 output energy. When electrons hit any intermediate temperature region in the cavity string, bursts of 

- 226 adsorbed hydrogen gas can be sometimes observed and trigger vacuum interlocks, which can also lead to 227 redistribution of the adsorbed gas in the cavity string and to lower operating gradients.

- 251 252 The electron loading in the SNS SRF cavities is from both field emission and multipacting, and some 253 cavities show radiation waveforms indicative of both multipacting and field emission. 254 255 Both high and medium beta cavities show the same indication of intense multipacting between 9 MV/m 256 and 14 MV/m. During an initial RF conditioning, for example after a cryomodule is thermally cycled, 257 multipacting usually starts from about 3 MV/m and such multipacting at low gradients is easily 258 conditioned out. The multipacting at higher gradients in contrast cannot be always completely conditioned 259 out and even is the main limiting factor for some cavities in the SCL. The resonant portion of the 260 electrons generated by multipacting are not always the ones inducing thermal instabilities because the 261 non-resonant secondary electrons can be strongly accelerated, especially in the high beta cavities, and be 262 responsible for the main thermal load and generation of radiation in the SNS cavities limited by 263 multipacting. - 320 The SNS FPC is designed and scaled from the one KEK developed for 508.8 MHz. Simple window 321 geometry was chosen to facilitate manufacturing and assembly. The design of the SNS FPC is based on a 322 simple 50 Ω coaxial line. The planar alumina window includes impedance-matching elements as well as a 323 TiN anti-multipacting coating. The chokes at both sides of the ceramic window have the important role of 324 improving the window matching [19]. The outer conductor of the FPC has a direct conduction path to 325 room temperature. To minimize heat transfer to the end group, the FPC outer conductor is designed to be 326 cooled by supercritical helium at 3 atm and 5 K with 0.04 g/s of mass flow rate, which removes about 30 327 W of static and dynamic heat load. The static heat load comes from the heat transfer between cryogenic 

- 328 temperature and ambient temperature, and the dynamic heat load refers to an additional heat load from RF 329 operation of the system. The inner surface of the outer conductor is designed to be copper-plated with 15 330 µm (RRR=10) on stainless steel to reduce wall dissipation. The inner conductor of the FPC is made of 331 copper and does not have active cooling in the vacuum side. The FPCs were tested up to 2-MW peak for 332 the full travelling wave condition in the test stand and over 550 kW peak power in real cavity operation at 333 various standing wave ratios limited by the operational envelope. Multipacting in the FPCs are also 334 observed, and mostly happens at the near full travelling wave condition during filling time, during cavity 335 field decay after RF is turned off or during cavity field flattop at high beam loading. The FPC is equipped 336 with an electron probe at the FPC ceramic window and the probe’s pulse waveforms are very useful to 337 monitor multipacting in the coupler region [20]. Aggressive multipacting is observed in some FPCs at 338 300 kW or above. Positive DC biasing of the inner conductor at around 800 V efficiently suppresses the 339 multipacting in those FPCs. 

- 341 Lorentz force detuning in SNS SRF cavities can be significant because the pulsed operation can 342 potentially drive mechanical resonance modes. The observed amount of dynamic detuning agrees quite 343 well with expectations in most of the cavities. The detuning coefficients are about 3-4 Hz/(MV/m)[2] and 1- 344 2 Hz/(MV/m)[2] in the medium- and high-beta cavities respectively. A few cavities show larger detuning 345 coefficients at the higher repetition rates, implying that mechanical resonances are excited in these 346 cavities. The amount of dynamic detuning of the high beta cavities during the RF flattop is less than 0.5 347 Hz/(MV/m)[2] . Since the cavity bandwidth is relatively large (~500 Hz) or the external Q is relatively low 348 (<10[6] ) due to the high beam loading, the additional RF power needed to compensate the dynamic 349 detuning is minor [21]. This amount of detuning is well managed by the adaptive feed forward 350 implemented in the SNS low level RF (LLRF) system. Fast piezo tuners were installed to mitigate any 351 unexpected mechanical resonance conditions. Up to now there is no strong need to utilize them and they 352 have never been operated.

364 Each cryomodule is functionally equipped with five valves; shield supply valve, primary Joule Thomson 365 (JT) valve, secondary JT valve, cool down valve, and primary return (RT) valve.  The shield supply valve 366 regulates the exiting temperature of the cryomodule shield. 378 The cryogenic system and cryomodule design closely resembles that of CEBAF at TJNAF.  One key 379 change made to the cryomodule design was to incorporate a heat exchanger in every cryomodule as 380 opposed to one large heat exchanger in the 2K cold box.  This change resulted in greater efficiency by 381 reducing the 2K envelope and absorbing the transfer line heat at the 4K level rather than the 2K level. 382 The system is primarily operated by the automated sequences which were developed in the initial 383 cryogenic system commissioning time frame of 2004-2005. The control system is equipped with an alarm 384 auto-dialer that calls a cryogenic engineer in the event of an alarm. Currently, the cryogenic system is 385 only manned one shift per day for minor adjustment of processing parameters. 386 387 After developing much of the control of the system manually, automated sequences were developed to 388 assist the operator in operating the plant.  The main sequences are backfill, 4-K pump down, 2-K pump 389 down, and the heater control sequence.  Figure 6 describes the function of each of these sequences. In 390 addition to these sequences, a 2-K cold box trip sequence is important to the operation of the SCL.  In the 391 event of a trip of the 2-K cold box, a trip sequence was developed to protect the cryomodules during this 392 event. 393 394 395 **Insert Figure 6** 396 397 398 When transitioning from 4-K to 2-K operation, the first sequence is the ‘Backfill’ sequence. The term 399 ‘backfill’ refers to flowing helium gas into the return transfer line and raising the pressure of the 400 cryomodules to a defined value.  In the SNS ‘Backfill’ sequence, cold helium gas flows from the liquid 401 helium Dewar to the return transfer line. This sequence prepares the 2-K cold box for pump down by 402 backfilling the Linac to around 1.1 bar, cooling the 2-K cold box down, establishing the correct flow path, 403 and starting all four cold compressors at a minimum speed. Upon successful completion of backfill, the 404 ‘4-K Pump Down’ sequence is activated. This sequence controls the 4.5-K cold box and cryomodules 405 during the pump down. All three of the other cold compressors are controlled by preset gear ratios within 408 the sequence. - 606 607 Operational statistics indicate that the performance degradation of the SNS SRF cavities is mostly related 608 to vacuum activity that can be triggered by errant beam, beam halo, electron activity and gas bursts from 609 ion pumps. Sometimes, arcing or discharge following the vacuum activity can damage the cavity surface 610 or ancillary equipment such as the FPCs and RF feedthroughs. 611 612 The term ‘errant beam’ hereafter refers to an off-energy beam generated anywhere in the accelerator, and 613 transported downstream in a fault condition; this is different from the continuous uncontrolled beam loss 614 in a normal operating condition. Since the errant beam is an off-energy beam, it is mostly lost during 615 transport through the Linac. This results in significantly higher beam loss in the Linac, which triggers 616 multiple BLM trips and consequently beam trips. During an errant beam condition in the SNS Linac, the 617 beam loss region typically ranges from several to ten cryomodules in length. Two mechanisms have been 618 identified as sources of errant beams at the SNS: (1) RF truncations in upstream RF structures and (2) 619 abnormal beam generation in the front end, which includes the ion source, the low energy beam transport 620 system, and the RF quadrupole [29]. 621 

- 622 Each RF structure has its own fast interlocks, which truncate an RF pulse when a fault condition is 623 detected, such as a vacuum burst, discharge, arc, or any abnormal RF signal. These interlocks trigger the 624 machine protection system, which aborts the beam. There is a delay time for the machine protection 625 system to abort the beam, as illustrated in figure 12. When an interlock of any RF structure truncates its 626 RF pulse, the beam from the start of the RF truncation until the beam abortion by the machine protection 627 system will be an off-energy beam. The second type of errant beam arises from unusual conditions in the 628 front-end system, such as a lower-than-nominal current beam pulse, a partial beam pulse or another 629 abnormal beam condition arising from high-voltage arcing, unstable ion source plasma, or mis-triggered 630 RF for plasma generation. The beam under these conditions becomes an off-energy beam while it is 631 accelerated, since the RF adaptive feed forward is only for the nominal beam pulse. In this case, beam 632 loss monitors are the first indicators of the event. The machine protection system delay is the same as for 633 the RF truncation case. The original requirement of the machine protection system delay at the SNS is 634 30 µs.

- 801 The SNS SCL with all related supporting systems is currently providing stable operation for the neutron 802 production up to 1.4-MW beam power on target with a high availability. - 812 One of the most severe events for the SRF cavity performance degradation is arcing or discharges. Once 813 an irreversible damage occurs after arcing or discharge, performance of a SRF cavity would get worse 814 over time. In this context, first turn-on must be closely watched and controlled since there is an associated 815 risk to create irreversible damage of RF surfaces, HOM couplers, and FPCs. First turn on includes initial 816 powering of cavities in a cryomodule, exploring limiting gradients, and increasing repetition rate. During 817 first turn-on, most cavities show aggressive multipacting and sometimes bursts of field emitters that can 818 potentially lead to arcing and a discharge condition. Similar situations may occur when cavities are turned 819 on after long maintenance periods or after thermal cycling. Careful RF conditioning is then required since 820 the behavior of a cavity can be significantly altered due to redistribution of gas on the RF surfaces. 

- 822 Root causes for performance degradation of the SRF cavities at SNS during operation have been 823 identified. Early diagnostics and prompt involvement from system experts are critical to minimize 824 additional degradation. No systematic performance degradation of SRF cavity performances has been 825 observed at the SNS.  Continuous efforts to develop dedicated diagnostics and more performant 

826 protection systems have contributed to further reduce the occurrence of performance degradation during 827 operation. Recovery actions including RF conditioning, thermal cycling and offline repair are actively 828 ongoing to sustain the SCL performance.
