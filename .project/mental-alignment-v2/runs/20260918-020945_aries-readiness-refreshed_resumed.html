Model results and ARIES comparison plan

What the model does

A computer model now finds several nearby reactor designs that pass its engineering checks. That is progress toward a plausible design, but it is not proof that the plant could be built or produce electricity at the estimated price.

The model calculates plant performance and cost from choices such as reactor size, magnet current, plasma temperature and cooling equipment. It began with Stellaris, a published stellarator design—a type of fusion reactor that uses magnetic fields to hold hot plasma. ARIES-CS is another published design, kept aside so we can later test the model against information that did not guide its development.

The latest study changed the design choices and checked the results against twenty modeled limits: for example, whether magnetic field stays below its limit, conductor fits and carries the required current, and heating and cooling capacity are sufficient. Power values must also pass a separate validity check before a design can count as passing. It did not change the equations or make the limits easier to pass. The two saved Stellaris test cases still fail some checks; the new passing designs use different inputs.

One passing design was selected as the center for tests of small design changes. The study calls it the anchor. Its estimated electricity cost is $150.43/MWh; it was chosen to leave room below engineering limits, not to find the lowest price.

Latest study results

An earlier search found no design passing every check among 71 selected cases plus a baseline calculation. The latest search also varied the plasma’s peak density and temperature, alongside reactor dimensions and magnet current. Those changes affect fusion power, heating demand and the heat that the plant must remove.

The selected design produces a modeled 1,010.112 MW of net electricity, after subtracting the electricity used inside the plant. It has 18 helium cooling circuits, which carry heat through the cooling system. It also buys 1% more superconducting conductor than the magnet-current sizing calculation requires. The study writes that purchase factor as 1.01. The extra conductor costs money and takes up space; it is not an assumption that the material performs better.

The three counts answer different questions

103 of 334 is the result across the study’s selected designs, including several sets of changed assumptions and the saved test cases. 44 of 256 is the result in the particular two-input plot above; the other marks are 210 failures and 2 invalid power calculations. 43 of 45 is the result of small changes around the selected design. These sets overlap and must not be added together.

The selected design is not the cheapest passing point even in this plot. The study sought room below engineering limits, not a minimum-cost plant.

Why small changes can turn a pass into a failure

A design has to satisfy several limits at once. Making the reactor smaller at the same coil current raises the peak magnetic field. Making it larger changes the field and the plasma’s ability to retain heat, which changes the required heating and exhaust heat. The divertor is the part that receives and removes plasma exhaust heat; its modeled limit is 10 MW per square meter.

Some passes are close to failure

Among passing nearby designs, the smallest spare allowances are 0.026 T for field and 0.032 MW/m² for divertor heat.

This spare allowance is the margin: the distance between the calculated result and its limit. The study found passing changes on both sides of each of the seven continuous inputs, at 17 and 19 cooling circuits, and in all sixteen tested combinations of changes. That demonstrates more than a single lucky point, but it does not prove every design between the tested points passes.

What the conductor allowance and cooling circuits establish

At the same selected design with no extra conductor—purchase factor 1.0—the computer model still reports a current-related result infinitesimally on the failing side of its limit. The extra 1% is a separate physical purchase choice; the failing result is preserved.

Eighteen cooling circuits imply 36 representative circulators and 18 intermediate heat exchangers. A circulator moves coolant; a heat exchanger transfers heat between circuits. Total calculated exchanger duty is 3,013.915 MW, or 167.440 MW per circuit. These are required capacities, not proof that particular equipment can provide them or a quote for installed equipment. A separate calculation with 14 circuits also passes, so eighteen has not been shown to be the best number.

Passing every current check still leaves important plant questions

The reactor needs to produce enough tritium fuel to replace what it consumes and loses. Its tritium breeding ratio is tritium produced relative to tritium consumed. The model assumes an achieved ratio of 1.074, above its pass/fail rule’s 1.05 minimum. But its separate fuel-cycle calculation requires 1.190. The check passes without showing that the plant can supply its own tritium.

The selected design’s peak field also exceeds the conductor measurements’ approximate 24 T extent. The study does not establish a new self-consistent magnetic configuration or a manufacturable coil design. Larger coil spaces, conductor manufacture and added cooling equipment remain incompletely priced. Costs also use mixed price years and assumed financing and maintenance. The reported LCOE is therefore an estimate under those assumptions, not a complete plant price.

Comparison with Stellaris

Finding new passing designs does not change the results for the original inputs. Before this search, the project saved two Stellaris test cases and their results in the second archived comparison package, called r2. Keeping those cases unchanged lets us check that the model still produces the same answer when given the same inputs. The package is “frozen” in the sense that its saved software, inputs and comparison rules will not be tuned after seeing ARIES; it does not refer to physical hardware.

Model assessment

We have a rubric to assess how much of the plant the model calculates, how its calculations respond to design changes, and how detailed its costs are. This is different from asking whether a particular design passes the engineering checks.

The rubric

Each plant area receives separate physics and structure/cost scores. A score is the highest level for which all required evidence exists. A target is the level the project expects that plant area to reach; level four is not required everywhere. Physics and cost scores are never averaged.

1A value is assumed or supplied; changing the design does not recalculate it.A named subsystem has one aggregate cost estimate.
2The value is calculated from design inputs and the executable calculation is checked.Costs follow calculated quantities such as size or power, with identified sources and accounts.
3A calculated value is tested against a limit, and the subsystem affects plant operation or whether the design passes.Components are sized separately, with appropriate purchase, installation, replacement and maintenance costs.
4Interacting physics are solved across subsystem boundaries, independently checked and exercised over a justified design range or uncertainty model.An estimate is tied to the engineered design, covers procurement through maintenance, states uncertainty or maturity, and is checked against a suitable reference.

Remaining gaps

  • Achieved tritium production: calculate what the blanket produces instead of assuming a breeding ratio.
  • Cooling-system costs: price installed pumps, pipes and heat exchangers individually.
  • Buildings: derive building and shielded maintenance-facility sizes from the plant layout.
  • Fuel inventory: calculate the stock required for operation and startup.
  • Fuel-processing costs: size and price equipment from how much fuel it must handle.
  • Estimate quality: state how mature the cost estimate is and quantify its uncertainty.

The new passing designs do not fill these missing calculations. For example, assuming tritium production exceeds a chosen floor can pass a check while the physics score for calculating that production remains below target.

Model evolution

The early model linked physics estimates to costs. Later work added calculations that connect design choices to equipment, limits and electricity production:

  • Magnet current changes conductor needs. More required conductor raises purchase cost and may no longer fit in the allocated coil space.
  • Moving coolant uses electricity. Required flow changes pumping power, leaving less electricity to sell. Coolant temperature also affects the fraction of heat converted to electricity.
  • Parts wearing out reduces production. Calculated component life changes replacement dates, downtime and lifetime electricity output.
  • Heat removal limits the design. Divertor heat calculations and individual cooling-circuit capacities expose demands that one total-power estimate would hide.

Later comparison with Stellaris clarified which numbers were supplied from the paper and which were independent calculations. That work explained several differences but left ignition unreproduced.

Calculation checks

The archived comparison package passed an independent reproduction review and 97 tests after extraction. Its report retains 174 comparison entries, including quantities the model does not produce. Those focused tests do not erase earlier failures in broader validation tests.

The study runs a Python executable generated from the model. A second Python implementation separately calculates the same equations and compares the results. Project records call these the native executable and the oracle. Because they share equations and assumptions, agreement is evidence of correct implementation, not proof that those assumptions describe a real plant.

Calculated numbers

75,484 compared numeric values meet the retained combined absolute/relative error criterion.

Pass/fail decisions

6,680 decisions reconstructed from the executable’s own numbers agree with its reported results.

Saved test cases

Both Stellaris cases reproduce all 242 saved numeric outputs and 20 pass/fail results exactly.

A known boundary difference

Six relative-error differences near zero and three current-related pass/fail disagreements remain when no extra conductor is purchased.

Near zero, tiny numerical differences can put two implementations on opposite sides of a limit. The study retains those failures. The general strict verification tool stopped at that known boundary; a separately labeled comparison of all cases records the agreement and exceptions. Sixteen numeric outputs have no counterpart in the second implementation. Independent review accepted the result with those limits stated.

ARIES comparison plan

The original aim was “80% methodology demo”: show that a structured engineering model written in SysML v2 could generate working calculation software, then test whether its physics and cost calculations apply beyond its starting design. The wider motivation was to understand what would need to be true for electricity costing 1¢/kWh.

Stellaris provided the starting published design, alongside the W7-X stellarator experiment and generic magnetic-fusion sources. It did not provide a complete cost benchmark. The separate 1costingFE costing library checks the accounting machinery when given matching inputs. ARIES-CS supplies the more demanding test: how well do the model’s predictions agree with another design’s reported quantities and costs?

Holding the ARIES papers aside avoids building the model to match the answers it will later be judged against. This is one published design comparison, not a statistical test set. The first run will calculate that reported design point; it will not search for a better-fitting design.

The comparison package is approved; opening the papers is a separate decision

The owner has approved the saved r2 package and a first comparison using its helium cooling assumptions. Any unsupported match between that cooling system and the reference must remain visible. The latest feasibility study is closed. The owner still decides when to open the ARIES papers, and a different archived model would require separate approval.

01

Open and record the reference papers

After the owner authorizes reveal, record the date and which files were opened. Keep extracted values with their sources in the designated comparison records.

02

Replace only the seven permitted design inputs

Use the paper’s major and minor plasma radii, peak density and temperature, coil current-times-turns, and two dimensions allocating space around the coil. Record units and check that each quantity means the same thing in the paper and the model. Missing values remain visibly assumed; affected outputs cannot be claimed to represent the reported design exactly.

03

Run the saved calculation without tuning it to match

Use the archived Python executable. Keep its density/temperature shapes, no-extra-conductor setting, fourteen cooling circuits and remaining assumptions. Preserve calculated outputs, all twenty pass/fail results and execution failures.

04

Compare like quantities under the agreed rules

Check whether the same major subsystems are represented, whether layers around the plasma are ordered consistently, and whether cost accounts cover the same equipment. For calculated physical quantities, the model/reference ratio must be between one-third and three. For component costs it must be between one-half and two. Missing or incompatible quantities remain visible, not passes.

05

Preserve the first report, then investigate differences separately

Do not overwrite the first prediction with an improved match. Later tests may supply selected reference outputs to isolate a downstream calculation; those supplied values earn no independent prediction credit. Corrections, different technologies and design optimization belong in separately identified versions.

Additional comparison rules that affect interpretation

The LCOE estimate is supporting information, with no formal pass range. Some cost inputs use different price years; the agreed formal comparison does not permit adjusting them to a common year. Any later price-year or finance adjustment is a separately labeled comparison.

The inherited power-supply cost account C220107 contains ARIES-derived information. It must be excluded or footnoted, including its effect on totals, rather than counted as an independent prediction. The special Table 5 supplied-value run remains a Stellaris check, not a way to substitute ARIES geometry. The preparation summary describes the published package; it does not amend it.

A favorable comparison and an engineering pass are different results. Agreement with a reported cost cannot erase a failed heat limit. Likewise, similar numerical values cannot establish that two different cooling technologies are equivalent.

Assessment

The latest study shows that changing the modeled design can produce several nearby cases that pass the current checks. That makes the ARIES test more informative. It does not make the two original Stellaris cases pass, reproduce published ignition, supply the six missing model capabilities or qualify a plant for construction. The owner has closed this study; opening ARIES remains a separate decision.
