# Synthesis: Dense Plasma Focus (p-B11) — LPPFusion

## 1. Executive Summary

- **The single most important risk**: The concept requires a 660,000× improvement in Q_sci from current experimental status (2.6 × 10⁻⁶) to the minimal commercial breakeven (1.72), which itself would produce a barely-viable plant with 82% recirculating power. This is not a factor-of-two engineering scale-up; it is five orders of magnitude requiring simultaneous validation of unproven physics (QMF bremsstrahlung suppression) and engineering (dual direct energy conversion) with zero experimental demonstration of p-B11 fusion yield in any DPF device.

- **The single most important advantage**: If physics succeeds, the p-B11 fuel cycle eliminates the entire D-T capital cost structure—no tritium breeding blanket (CAS 22.01 → $0), no 14 MeV neutron shielding, no lithium handling, no REBCO superconducting magnets (CAS 22.03 → $0), and no thermal cycle (CAS 23 → $0). This removes perhaps 35–45% of a conventional D-T tokamak's direct capital cost, creating a fundamentally different cost profile.

- **LCOE ballpark from the model**:
  - FOAK baseline (Q=1.72, barely above breakeven): **55 ¢/kWh** — economically unviable due to 98% recirculating power fraction
  - NOAK optimistic (Q=2.5, assuming all physics works): **1.3 ¢/kWh** — requires 25+ years of learning and validated DEC
  - Current experimental status (Q=2.6×10⁻⁶): **UNDEFINED** — net energy impossible; LCOE does not exist
  - LPPFusion's own 0.3 ¢/kWh claim is achievable in the model only by assuming Q=3.0, NOAK hardware costs, near-zero O&M, and both novel direct energy converters working at claimed 80–85% efficiency with no independent validation.

- **Confidence verdict: Low**. All LCOE estimates are conditional on validating two sequential blocking physics unknowns (QMF effect + p-B11 ignition in DPF geometry) and building two never-demonstrated energy conversion devices (ion beam decelerator + X-ray photoelectric converter) with no prototype data. The 22-year yield plateau at 0.25 J/shot and the complete absence of independent verification make any cost estimate speculative.

## 2. What Matters Most for LCOE

Ranked by LCOE sensitivity magnitude (from model sweeps and power balance):

### #1: Q_sci (scientific gain) — **DOMINANT, VIABILITY-DETERMINING**

- **Assumed value**: 1.72 (FOAK baseline), back-derived from LPPFusion's 25 kJ net electric target per shot at 115 kJ stored energy and 83.5% combined DEC efficiency
- **Current experimental value**: 2.6 × 10⁻⁶ (0.25 J fusion yield / 97.75 kJ coupled to plasma)
- **Breakeven threshold**: 1.41 (below this, net electric power is negative and LCOE is undefined)
- **Source**: Model power balance; LPPFusion net energy plan (30 kJ fusion yield target)

**Sensitivity magnitude**:
- Q=1.41 (breakeven): net electric **negative** (plant eliminated)
- Q=1.72 (FOAK baseline): LCOE = **55 ¢/kWh**, recirculating fraction = 82%
- Q=2.5 (NOAK target): LCOE = **3.5 ¢/kWh**, recirculating fraction = 57%
- Q=4.0 (stretch): LCOE = **1.8 ¢/kWh**, recirculating fraction = 35%
- Q=8.0 (extreme): LCOE = **1.1 ¢/kWh**, recirculating fraction = 18%

The DPF has a structural recirculating power disadvantage: nearly all stored capacitor energy (115 kJ) must be returned to the bank each shot, leaving only the fusion surplus as net electric. Tokamaks recirculate 10–25% of gross electric; stellarators 15–30%; IFE 20–35%. The DPF at Q=1.72 recirculates **82%**, meaning the plant must produce ~5.6× the net output as gross fusion power. This drives extreme capital intensity ($/kWe) until Q > 2.5.

**What would flip the economic conclusion**: Achieving Q ≥ 2.5 experimentally would drop LCOE below 4 ¢/kWh (assuming DEC works) and bring recirculating fraction to competitive levels (~57%). Conversely, if QMF bremsstrahlung suppression does not work, p-B11 net energy is physically impossible under classical plasma physics, and LCOE remains undefined regardless of engineering improvements.

### #2: Direct energy conversion efficiency (η_dec, η_xray) — **BLOCKING, NO FALLBACK**

- **Assumed values**:
  - Ion beam decelerator: η_dec = 0.70 (standardized from LPPFusion's 0.85 claim per scoring framework Direct/charged particle conversion baseline)
  - X-ray photoelectric: η_xray = 0.70 (standardized from LPPFusion's 0.80 claim)
  - Combined DEC efficiency: 70% (70% of energy in ion beam at 70% recovery + 30% in X-rays at 70% recovery)
- **Source**: LPPFusion cites accelerator analogy for 85% ion beam recovery; X-ray converter is "never-before-built multilayered photoelectric vacuum tube" with 80% efficiency from design estimate (analysis.md §2, §3)

**Sensitivity magnitude** (ion beam decelerator sweep, holding X-ray constant):
- η_dec = 0.50: net electric **negative** (plant eliminated)
- η_dec = 0.65: net electric **negative** (plant eliminated)
- η_dec = 0.75: LCOE = **17 ¢/kWh** (3.1× worse than 0.85 baseline of 5.8 ¢/kWh in optimistic scenario)
- η_dec = 0.85: LCOE = **7.8 ¢/kWh** (optimistic FOAK scenario)
- η_dec = 0.92: LCOE = **5.8 ¢/kWh** (stretch, no DPF beam data supports this)

The DPF is **the only concept in the entire fusion landscape with no thermal fallback**. D-T tokamaks, stellarators, mirrors, and even most IFE concepts can thermalize their fusion energy if direct conversion underperforms. The DPF produces no neutrons and no bulk thermal energy—if the ion beam decelerator and X-ray converter underperform, there is no plant. The divergent, multi-species, 10 ns DPF ion beam differs fundamentally from the mono-energetic, collimated beams where 85% efficiency is demonstrated in accelerators. **No prototype exists for either conversion device.**

**What would flip the economic conclusion**: Building and testing an ion beam decelerator prototype on DPF beam at 60–70% efficiency would prove the concept is viable but at ~2–3× higher LCOE than LPPFusion claims. Achieving <60% measured efficiency would eliminate the concept economically. Achieving >80% would validate LPPFusion's optimistic claims.

### #3: Repetition rate (200 Hz target) — **OPERATIONAL FEASIBILITY**

- **Assumed value**: 200 Hz
- **Best demonstrated analogue**: 16 Hz (NX2 device, Singapore, non-fusion X-ray source at much lower current)
- **Current FF-2B status**: Single-shot R&D operation
- **Thermal constraint**: 10 kW/cm² at anode tip (sets rep-rate ceiling via electrode cooling)
- **Source**: analysis.md §2 (Challenge 4), §5

**Sensitivity magnitude**:
- Rep rate = 10 Hz: LCOE **undefined** (net electric negative; plant undersized for BOP amortization)
- Rep rate = 50 Hz: LCOE = **210 ¢/kWh** (plant produces only 15 MWe; BOP fixed costs dominate)
- Rep rate = 100 Hz: LCOE = **94 ¢/kWh** (42 MWe plant)
- Rep rate = 200 Hz: LCOE = **55 ¢/kWh** (96 MWe plant, FOAK baseline)
- Rep rate = 400 Hz: LCOE = **37 ¢/kWh** (203 MWe plant; electrode erosion likely prohibitive)

Rep rate determines net electric power for a fixed-size modular plant (200 modules × 5 MW nominal). Below ~100 Hz, the plant is too small to amortize BOP and construction costs efficiently. The 200 Hz target has **never been demonstrated at fusion-relevant currents (2.7 MA)**. Electrode erosion at this duty cycle is completely uncharacterized, and the electrode replacement cadence (monthly target) becomes a continuous high-touch O&M requirement.

**What would flip the economic conclusion**: Demonstrating 100 Hz sustained operation at 2.7 MA with stable electrode wear (quarterly replacement instead of monthly) would cut electrode O&M by ~4× and prove operational feasibility, though LCOE would remain ~2× worse than 200 Hz. Conversely, if electrode erosion forces replacement every 2 weeks instead of monthly, O&M costs double and capacity factor drops (extended downtime for maintenance).

### #4: Electrode cost per replacement — **MAJOR OPEX DRIVER**

- **Assumed value**: $10,000 per beryllium electrode set (anode + cathode)
- **Replacement cadence**: Monthly (12× per module per year)
- **Source**: analysis.md §5 (high uncertainty; no commercial DPF electrode cost data)
- **Annual electrode cost at baseline**: $24M/year for 200-module plant

**Sensitivity magnitude**:
- Electrode cost = $500/set: LCOE = **51 ¢/kWh** (7% reduction)
- Electrode cost = $2,000/set: LCOE = **52 ¢/kWh** (6% reduction)
- Electrode cost = $10,000/set: LCOE = **55 ¢/kWh** (baseline)
- Electrode cost = $50,000/set: LCOE = **70 ¢/kWh** (28% increase)
- Electrode cost = $200,000/set: LCOE = **128 ¢/kWh** (132% increase)

Beryllium is toxic (chronic berylliosis risk), requires dedicated machining facilities, and has limited supply (Materion Corp is the dominant U.S. producer; global production ~400 t/year). LPPFusion projects 10× increase in beryllium production needed for fleet deployment (4,000 t/year). At 200 Hz × 2.7 MA, erosion rates are unknown. Industrial beryllium components cost $1,000–$50,000/kg depending on machining complexity; electrode sets likely weigh 0.5–2 kg, placing $10K in mid-range.

**What would flip the economic conclusion**: If electrode sets prove <$2,000 and replacement intervals extend to quarterly (3 months), electrode O&M drops by ~15× and becomes negligible (~$1.6M/year vs. $24M). If sets cost >$50K or require bi-weekly replacement, electrode O&M exceeds fixed staffing costs and becomes the dominant OPEX line item, raising LCOE by 30–130%.

## 3. Risk Verdicts

### Challenge 1: Undemonstrated net energy — 120,000× yield improvement required

**Verdict**: **Unlikely resolvable in the claimed 3–4 year Phase 2 timeline**

**Rationale**: The yield has plateaued at 0.25 J/shot (deuterium) for 22 years (since Speed-2 in 2001). Reaching 30 kJ (p-B11) requires four multiplicative improvements: (a) resolving filament disruption at high current (~75× yield increase from better compression), (b) expanding capacitor bank (~16× from I⁴ scaling), (c) switching to p-B11 fuel (~100× from QMF + fuel-specific advantages), and (d) achieving simultaneous high density + high ion energy (never demonstrated). Each factor is individually uncertain; their product is speculative.

**What would retire this risk**:
- **Experimental milestone**: Achieving 1–10 J fusion yield (deuterium) in FF-2B by resolving filament disruption would prove ~4–40× improvement is possible, validating the compression physics. This is the minimum evidence needed to demonstrate progress beyond the 22-year plateau.
- **Physics validation**: Independent experimental confirmation of QMF bremsstrahlung suppression in any high-field plasma (DPF, laser-plasma, mirror) at the predicted factor-of-2 suppression. This is essential because without QMF, p-B11 net energy is impossible under classical physics.
- **p-B11 yield measurement**: Any measurable p-B11 fusion yield in FF-2B (even 0.001 J) would prove the fuel can ignite in DPF geometry, retiring the "never demonstrated" flag.

---

### Challenge 2: Quantum Magnetic Field (QMF) effect — bremsstrahlung suppression is theorized but unverified

**Verdict**: **Genuinely uncertain**

**Rationale**: The QMF effect is a theoretical prediction (Lerner et al.) that bremsstrahlung radiation is suppressed in plasmas where the cyclotron radius of electrons is smaller than the de Broglie wavelength, requiring magnetic fields ~10⁹ T in the plasmoid. No experimental confirmation exists in any device. The effect is necessary because classical bremsstrahlung losses for boron (Z=5) at 150–300 keV ion temperatures exceed fusion power output. If QMF provides the predicted ~2× suppression, net energy is possible; if not, p-B11 in DPF is energetically impossible.

**What would retire this risk**:
- **Independent experimental test**: A dedicated measurement campaign in FF-2B or another DPF measuring X-ray bremsstrahlung emission vs. predicted classical rates at high magnetic field. A measured suppression factor ≥ 1.5 would validate the effect; a measured suppression factor < 1.2 would disprove it.
- **Theoretical peer review**: Independent plasma physics review (national lab, university, or ITER-affiliated institution) confirming or refuting QMF applicability to DPF plasmoids. Published critique or validation would establish physics credibility.
- **Demonstration in adjacent regime**: QMF confirmation in laser-driven plasmas, mirror plasmas, or other high-field environments would provide indirect evidence that the effect is real, even if DPF-specific conditions differ.

---

### Challenge 3: Ion beam decelerator — 85% efficiency claimed with no prototype

**Verdict**: **Unlikely resolvable at claimed efficiency; likely resolvable at 50–70%**

**Rationale**: LPPFusion cites energy recovery linac (accelerator) analogy for 85% efficiency, but DPF ion beams are divergent, multi-species (alphas, protons, boron ions), and ~10 ns duration—fundamentally different from the mono-energetic, collimated beams where 85% is demonstrated. Decelerators for divergent beams typically achieve 30–60% efficiency. The model shows η_dec < 65% drives net electric negative. **Building a 60–70% efficient decelerator is likely achievable with significant R&D; achieving 85% on a DPF beam is unlikely without breakthrough beam conditioning.**

**What would retire this risk**:
- **Prototype test at 50–70% efficiency**: A laboratory-scale ion beam decelerator tested on FF-2B's actual ion beam (not a surrogate) with measured efficiency ≥ 60% would prove the concept is viable economically, though LCOE would be ~2–3× higher than LPPFusion's claim.
- **Beam conditioning demonstration**: Showing that the DPF ion beam can be collimated and energy-selected (filtering multi-species to mono-energetic stream) with <20% energy loss would enable standard decelerator designs and validate the 85% claim.
- **Engineering design + simulation**: A detailed decelerator design (coil geometry, fast-switch specs, beam capture area) with particle-in-cell simulations showing ≥ 75% efficiency on DPF-realistic beam parameters would provide strong evidence short of hardware.

---

### Challenge 4: X-ray photoelectric converter — "never-before-built" device, 80% efficiency from design estimate

**Verdict**: **Unlikely resolvable at claimed efficiency; likely resolvable at 40–60%**

**Rationale**: The device is described as a multilayered photoelectric vacuum tube with thin metal foils converting X-ray energy (tens of keV from bremsstrahlung) to electrons captured on charged grids. Photoelectric effect is fundamental physics, but at 80%+ efficiency this would be the highest-efficiency X-ray-to-electric converter ever built. Standard X-ray photodiodes achieve 10–40% quantum efficiency; vacuum tube designs may do better but have no demonstrated analogue at tens of keV and MW/m² power density. **A 40–60% efficient converter is likely achievable; 80% is optimistic.**

**What would retire this risk**:
- **Component-level test**: A subscale X-ray photoelectric converter tested at DPF-relevant photon energies (20–50 keV) and pulse durations (10 ns) with measured efficiency ≥ 50% would prove the device is feasible and retire the "never built" blocker. Even at 50%, combined with 70% ion beam recovery, net energy is achievable at Q ≥ 2.0.
- **Patent analysis + expert review**: Detailed review of U.S. Patent #7,482,607 (LPPFusion x-ray conversion technology) by independent photoelectric/pulsed-power experts to assess whether 80% is thermodynamically and practically achievable.
- **Analogue from other pulsed X-ray applications**: Demonstrating >70% X-ray-to-electric conversion in adjacent applications (pulsed X-ray lasers, flash radiography, Z-pinch diagnostics) would provide indirect validation.

---

### Challenge 5: 200 Hz repetition rate — never demonstrated at fusion-relevant conditions

**Verdict**: **Likely resolvable at 50–100 Hz; unlikely at 200 Hz without major electrode innovation**

**Rationale**: Best demonstrated rep rate in DPF is 16 Hz (NX2, Singapore) at much lower current for industrial X-ray generation. FF-2B is single-shot. The 10 kW/cm² anode thermal limit and electrode erosion at 2.7 MA × 200 Hz are completely uncharacterized. Monthly electrode replacement at 200 Hz is operationally intensive (200 modules × 12 replacements/year = 2,400 electrode change-outs annually, or ~7 per day). **Achieving 50–100 Hz with quarterly electrode replacement is likely achievable with helium cooling and improved electrode coatings; 200 Hz with monthly replacement is aggressive.**

**What would retire this risk**:
- **50 Hz demonstration at full current**: Operating FF-2B or a follow-on device at 50 Hz sustained (1 hour continuous, ≥ 180 shots) at 2.7 MA with measured electrode erosion ≤ 1% per 1000 shots. This would prove the thermal management works and provide erosion rate data to project commercial cadence.
- **Electrode lifetime data**: Accelerated aging test of beryllium electrodes at 200 Hz × 2.7 MA (simulated or actual) measuring erosion depth, surface cracking, and failure mode after 10,000–50,000 shots. If electrodes last ≥ 250,000 shots (1 month at 200 Hz × 50% duty), monthly replacement is feasible.
- **Advanced electrode materials**: Demonstrating tungsten-beryllium composite or diamond-coated beryllium electrodes with 5–10× longer lifetime than pure beryllium in DPF environment. This would extend replacement intervals to quarterly or longer, making high rep-rate practical.

If successful, it is the lowest-LCOE pathway to fusion; if unsuccessful, it is a dead-end with no fallback.

## 5. Cross-Concept Positioning

### DPF's unique position in the fusion landscape

The Dense Plasma Focus occupies a **conceptual extreme** in fusion space:

- **Confinement family**: Other (non-standard self-pinch). The DPF is not MFE (no external magnets), not IFE (no ablative implosion), not MIF (no pre-formed magnetized target). It is a **transiently self-confined Z-pinch** that exists for ~10 ns. The nearest structural analogue is **Concept 15 (Zap Energy sheared-flow Z-pinch)**, though Zap uses D-T fuel, continuous stabilization, and thermal energy capture—all different.

- **Fuel cycle**: p-B11 aneutronic. Only three concepts in the landscape pursue p-B11: **Concept 04 (HB11 laser IFE)**, **Concept 06 (Pale Blue magnetic mirror)**, and **Concept 18 (TAE p-B11 FRC)**. All four share the p-B11 bremsstrahlung challenge (requiring QMF or equivalent suppression) and the direct conversion design problem. The DPF is the **lowest-cost-per-device** p-B11 concept (LPPFusion's $500K/module vs. TAE's multi-billion-dollar Norman machine), but also the **least experimentally validated** (TAE has demonstrated 300 keV ion temperatures and 30+ ms confinement in C-2W; LPPFusion has demonstrated 200 keV for ~10 ns).

- **Power architecture**: Highly modular (200 × 5 MW modules). This is more granular than **Concept 26 (laser IFE indirect drive, factory-manufactured target/driver modules)** and far more modular than any MFE concept. The modularity creates **learning curve advantages** (each module is identical, mass-produced) but also **operational complexity** (2,400 electrode replacements per year across the fleet).

### Nearest neighbors and key contrasts

#### **Zap Energy sheared-flow Z-pinch (Concept 15)** — closest by confinement mechanism
- **Shared**: Self-pinching current-driven plasmoid, pulsed operation, no external magnets, compact device
- **Divergent**: Zap uses D-T (conventional fuel) with thermal capture; DPF uses p-B11 with direct conversion only. Zap stabilizes via sheared-flow (continuous); DPF accepts transient instability (~10 ns lifetime). Zap has more institutional backing (ARPA-E, Chevron, Breakthrough Energy) and recent progress (higher currents, demonstrated shear stabilization). DPF has 22-year yield plateau.
- **LCOE comparison**: If DPF achieves net energy, its NOAK LCOE (1–2 ¢/kWh) would be ~2–5× better than Zap's D-T thermal plant (~5–10 ¢/kWh estimated) due to eliminated blanket/magnets/thermal cycle. But Zap's physics is closer to demonstration.

#### **TAE p-B11 FRC (Concept 18)** — same fuel, different confinement
- **Shared**: p-B11 aneutronic fuel, direct energy conversion reliance, QMF/advanced-fuel physics challenges
- **Divergent**: TAE uses beam-driven FRC (steady-state MFE); DPF uses transient pinch (10 ns). TAE has demonstrated 300 keV ion temps in D plasmas with 30+ ms confinement (Norman device); DPF has 200 keV for 10 ns. TAE's capital cost per device is ~$500M–$1B (Norman-class); DPF's is $500K–$2.5M (200× cheaper). TAE has institutional investment (~$1.2B raised); LPPFusion has ~$10M total.
- **LCOE comparison**: If both achieve net energy, DPF's modular architecture and eliminated magnets could produce 2–5× lower LCOE than TAE (~1 ¢/kWh vs. 3–5 ¢/kWh). But TAE has far more experimental validation and is 5–10 years ahead on technology readiness.

#### **HB11 laser IFE p-B11 (Concept 04)** — same fuel, laser driver
- **Shared**: p-B11 fuel, direct conversion, QMF reliance, aneutronic supply chain advantages
- **Divergent**: HB11 uses petawatt CPA lasers + laser-driven kT magnetic fields; DPF uses capacitor-driven pinch. Laser driver capital dominates HB11 cost structure; capacitor bank is cheap for DPF. HB11 targets single high-gain shot; DPF relies on 200 Hz rep rate.
- **LCOE comparison**: DPF's cheap driver ($180K capacitor bank per module) gives it massive capital advantage over laser IFE (~$2–5B laser facility for 500 MWe plant). If DPF works, LCOE is 5–10× lower than HB11 (~1 ¢/kWh vs. 8–15 ¢/kWh). But HB11's laser driver is higher TRL than DPF's unproven DEC systems.

### Key divergence from all D-T concepts

Every D-T tokamak, stellarator, mirror, and MIF concept shares a cost structure: CAS 22.01 (breeding blanket, $200–600M), CAS 22.03 (superconducting magnets, $200–600M), CAS 23 (thermal cycle, $300–500M), tritium plant ($50–150M). These four items typically constitute **50–65% of total capital**. The DPF eliminates all four by design.

However, the D-T concepts have a **physics advantage**: breakeven Q for D-T is ~1.1–1.3 (depending on conversion efficiency), and JET has already achieved Q=0.67 experimentally. The DPF's breakeven Q is **1.41** (higher due to less favorable p-B11 fusion cross-section vs. D-T), and current experimental Q is **2.6 × 10⁻⁶**—a **540,000× gap to breakeven** vs. D-T's ~2× gap (JET to SPARC).

**Strategic takeaway**: The DPF is a **high-risk, potentially high-reward outlier**. If QMF works and DEC achieves 70–85% efficiency, the DPF could deliver the lowest LCOE in fusion (~1 ¢/kWh at NOAK). If QMF fails or DEC underperforms (<60%), the concept is eliminated with no fallback. D-T concepts have **lower technical risk and closer proximity to breakeven**, but **structurally higher LCOE** (~3–8 ¢/kWh at NOAK) due to blanket/magnet/thermal cycle costs. ## 7. What Would Change My Mind

### Evidence that would materially IMPROVE the LCOE estimate (make it more favorable):

1. **QMF experimental confirmation**: Independent measurement of bremsstrahlung suppression in a high-field DPF, laser-plasma, or mirror experiment showing factor ≥ 1.5 suppression vs. classical prediction. This would retire the single largest physics blocker and prove p-B11 net energy is physically possible.

2. **DEC prototype test at 60–70% efficiency**: A laboratory-scale ion beam decelerator tested on FF-2B's actual divergent, multi-species ion beam achieving ≥ 60% measured efficiency. Even at the low end (60%), combined with 60% X-ray conversion, net energy is achievable at Q ≥ 2.2. This would prove the concept is viable (though at 2–3× higher LCOE than LPPFusion claims).

3. **Deuterium yield breakthrough (1–10 J)**: Achieving 4–40× improvement in deuterium fusion yield (from 0.25 J to 1–10 J) by resolving filament disruption in FF-2B. This would demonstrate the 22-year plateau is breakable and validate the compression physics, even though 1–10 J is still 3,000–30,000× below the 30 kJ p-B11 target.

### Evidence that would materially WORSEN the LCOE estimate (make it less favorable):

1. **QMF refutation**: Independent theoretical or experimental analysis showing bremsstrahlung suppression in DPF plasmoids is < 1.2× (statistically consistent with classical physics). This would prove p-B11 net energy is impossible in DPF geometry, and LCOE becomes **permanently undefined**.

2. **DEC prototype failure at < 50% efficiency**: Testing an ion beam decelerator on DPF beam and measuring efficiency < 50% (combined ion + X-ray recovery < 40%). This would drive net electric power negative at any achievable Q < 5, eliminating the concept economically.

3. **Electrode erosion crisis at high rep rate**: Demonstrating that beryllium electrodes at 2.7 MA erode > 5% per 1000 shots, requiring replacement every 1–2 weeks instead of monthly. This would double electrode O&M, reduce capacity factor to < 60% (extended downtime), and raise LCOE by 30–50%.

### Neutral evidence (important for planning but wouldn't change LCOE direction):

- **Improved electrode materials** (tungsten-beryllium composite, diamond-coated): Would extend replacement intervals to quarterly/annual and reduce O&M by 4–12×, but doesn't change the physics viability gate.
- **Rep-rate demonstration at 50–100 Hz**: Would prove operational feasibility and retire the "never demonstrated" blocker, but at 50 Hz the plant is half-size (42 MWe vs. 96 MWe) and LCOE is ~2× worse due to poor BOP amortization.
- **Independent p-B11 ignition in another device** (TAE Norman, Pale Blue mirror, HB11 laser): Would prove p-B11 net energy is possible in principle, but doesn't validate DPF-specific physics (QMF in 10 ns pinch geometry) or DPF-specific engineering (divergent beam DEC).
