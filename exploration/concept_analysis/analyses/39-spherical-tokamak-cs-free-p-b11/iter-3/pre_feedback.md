### F-1: Li (2024) comment paper is now available — quantifies hot ion mode infeasibility more severely than Section 2 implies
- **Target:** Section 2.1 (p-B11 ignition physics) and Section 6 Gap #8
- **Category:** analysis
- **Finding:** The analysis identifies Gap #8 as the comment on ENN's roadmap (pubs.aip.org/aip/pop/article/32/6/064701/3348211), noting "the specific critiques are not available in the extracted sources." The source at `iter-02/sources/arxiv-2406-15495.md` is that paper. Its specific critique is materially more severe than what Section 2 currently implies. Li (2024) calculates that under fusion self-heating, the achievable Ti/Te ratio is approximately 0.57 at Ti = 300 keV (not 4 as ENN's roadmap requires), and that maintaining Ti/Te = 4 would require external heating power equal to 5–20× the fusion power output. Section 2.1 frames the challenge as "the Lawson criterion may not be achievable thermally" — the Li result quantifies why: even if the plasma reaches sufficient temperature, sustaining the hot ion mode state requires external power that far exceeds the fusion output, making Q_engineering < 0 regardless of plasma confinement quality. This is distinct from the ECRH current drive concern discussed later in Section 2 (item 3).
- **Recommendation:** Add a paragraph to Section 2.1 specifically incorporating Li's finding: state that the achievable Ti/Te under fusion self-heating is bounded at ~0.57 (not the required ~4), that sustaining Ti/Te = 4 requires 5–20× fusion power in external heating, and that this means the hot ion mode approach carries a go/no-go physics gate that precedes all engineering challenges. Update Section 6 Gap #8 from "not-yet-sourced" to "resolved" and cite `iter-02/sources/arxiv-2406-15495.md` as the source.
- **Priority:** blocking

### F-2: Frontiers (2026) paper provides updated cross-section data with definitive Te/Ti operating windows — current analysis cites only Rider 1997 and Nevins 1998
- **Target:** Section 2.1 (p-B11 ignition physics) and Section 5 parameter table
- **Category:** analysis
- **Finding:** The analysis cites Rider (1997) and Nevins (1998) as the key physics references for the Lawson criterion. The Frontiers (2026) paper (`iter-02/sources/frontiersin-journals-nuclear-engineering-articles-10-3389.md`) provides a systematic update using Tentori-Belloni (2023) cross-section data (based on Sikora & Weller 2016, which covers a wider energy range and higher reactivity than Nevins-Swain 2000). Key findings not in the current analysis: (1) Te = Ti makes net p-B11 energy production **impossible across the entire 75–500 keV temperature range** — this is a more definitive statement than "may not be achievable thermally"; (2) the minimum achievable Lawson triple product with Te = 0.25Ti (Tentori-Belloni) is ~1.5 × 10²² m⁻³s at 270 keV, with a net-energy window of 125–330 keV — providing precise operating bounds for the first time in the new cross-section framework; (3) the Frontiers paper explicitly excludes synchrotron/cyclotron radiation from its loss model, noting this makes its Lawson conditions optimistic — meaning the actual thresholds are even harder than its figures show. The existing analysis mentions cyclotron radiation losses as a concern but does not connect this to the gap in the newer published physics.
- **Recommendation:** Update Section 2.1 to: (a) sharpen "may not be achievable thermally" to "impossible at Te = Ti across all temperatures (Tentori-Belloni 2023, Nevins-Swain 2000)" with appropriate citation; (b) add the net-energy operating window (125–330 keV at Te = 0.25Ti) as the more precise successor to the ">100–300 keV" figure currently in Section 5; (c) note that even this window excludes synchrotron radiation, so the actual requirement is harder still. Add a row to the Section 5 parameter table for the Lawson triple product minimum (~1.5 × 10²² m⁻³s at Te = 0.25Ti, Tentori-Belloni 2023) as the best current published lower bound, clearly marked as excluding cyclotron losses.
- **Priority:** important

### F-3: Li's heating power finding requires a new model scenario — hot ion mode infeasibility sits above and outside current H1/H2 framing
- **Target:** Section 2 Modeling Hypotheses and model_setup.py
- **Category:** model
- **Finding:** The current model hypotheses (H1: DEC failure, H2: ECRH recirculating power, H3: DEC-enabled competitiveness threshold) all assume that the p-B11 plasma state can be achieved and that Q > 1 is feasible if engineering challenges are met. Li's finding introduces a pre-engineering physics gate: if maintaining hot ion mode (Ti/Te = 4) requires external heating of 5–20× fusion power output, then Q_engineering is negative by a large margin regardless of DEC efficiency or ECRH current drive performance. This scenario — "hot ion mode heating burden" — is not captured by p_input (which models ECRH current drive power, not plasma state maintenance power) and is not captured by the DEC failure branch (which models conversion efficiency, not energy balance). It represents a distinct physics go/no-go that collapses the entire model rather than degrading LCOE by a factor.
- **Recommendation:** Add a fourth model hypothesis H4 — "Hot ion mode heating burden: if plasma state maintenance requires P_heating = k × P_fusion with k = 5–20 (from Li 2024), the model should evaluate net energy balance (P_fusion − P_heating − P_ECRH_drive) before any LCOE calculation is meaningful." Implement this in model_setup.py as a scenario flag `hot_ion_mode_heating_multiplier` (range 1–20, base case = 1 representing "physics solved") that scales an additional recirculating power term. At k ≥ 2, the net electrical output from direct conversion is negative regardless of η_de or p_input — confirm this boundary and report it as the conceptual go/no-go gate. This should be documented in the Modeling Hypotheses subsection of Section 2 alongside H1–H3.
- **Priority:** important

### F-1: DEC_failure scenario result contradicts analysis framing
- **Target:** Section 2 (Modeling Hypotheses, H1) and model DEC_failure branch
- **Category:** model
- **Finding:** The DEC_failure scenario shows +4% LCOE change (96.1 → 99.9 $/MWh), directly contradicting the analysis's characterization of DEC failure as having "catastrophic" economic impact and its statement that this is "the single most informative output this model can produce." The discrepancy is structural: the model scales fusion power at fixed geometry (2031 → 3270 MW, +61% fusion power, +5% overnight cost) to maintain 500 MWe net, so capital costs barely move. The economically informative scenario — holding fusion power constant at 2031 MW and showing net output collapse — is never computed. At constant fusion power with full fallback thermal (η_th = 0.35), gross electric output falls from ~1488 MWe to ~711 MWe; after ~500 MWe in parasitic loads (ECRH at 200 MW / η_pin = 0.5, coils, aux), net output collapses to ~200 MWe, and LCOE rises to ~230+ $/MWh. That is the scenario that demonstrates why DEC is go/no-go, not +4%.
- **Recommendation:** Add a constant-fusion-power DEC_failure variant alongside the existing scale-up variant: hold fusion at 2031 MW, set eta_de = 0.0, route all power through fallback thermal at η_th = 0.35, hold geometry fixed, and report the resulting net output (~200 MWe) and LCOE (~230 $/MWh). Label it "DEC_failure_fixed_fusion" and contrast it with the existing "DEC_failure_fixed_net" variant. The analysis framing of H1 should reference this variant as the primary demonstration and note that the scale-up variant (+4%) is technically valid but operationally misleading because it silently assumes the undemonstrated p-B11 Lawson criterion can be met at 60% higher fusion power in the same machine.
- **Priority:** blocking

### F-2: Copper coil recirculating power concern range not swept
- **Target:** Model sensitivity output and Section 2 (Challenge 3, CS-free operation)
- **Category:** model
- **Finding:** The model flags p_coils = 50 MW as "OPTIMISTIC — likely 5-10x higher" in the output header and the analysis (Section 3) states that a commercial-scale device with copper resistive coils could consume ~300 MW in coil ohmic heating — but no sweep of p_coils is shown. This is asymmetric: the ECRH p_input concern (200 → 700 MW) has a dedicated sweep table in the output, but the copper coil concern (50 → 500 MW) does not, despite the model explicitly flagging it as optimistic. At p_coils elasticity +0.019, the 5-10× concern range (50 → 500 MW) produces a ~17% LCOE impact (96 → ~113 $/MWh) — material enough to report. The analysis makes the HTS transition a pivotal unresolved question (Section 3, Section 7) but provides no numeric range for what the LCOE looks like in the copper-coil commercial-scale scenario.
- **Recommendation:** Add a p_coils sweep (50, 150, 300, 500 MW) to the model output, formatted in the same style as the p_input sweep, and label it "Copper coil recirculating power range (HTS-less scenario)." Report alongside the base LCOE and note that at p_coils > 300 MW, the recirculating power fraction from coils alone exceeds the ECRH concern midpoint, making the combined recirculating power fraction a compounding risk.
- **Priority:** important

- **Finding:** The sensitivity table shows `interest_rate: +nan`, a computation error. - **Recommendation:** Debug the interest_rate sensitivity computation in the model. If the elasticity cannot be computed analytically (e.g., due to compounding in the IDC formula), substitute a finite-difference approximation: compute LCOE at interest_rate ± 1 percentage point and report the resulting elasticity. - **Priority:** important
