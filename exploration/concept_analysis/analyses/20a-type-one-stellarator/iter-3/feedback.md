### F-1: Nearest-neighbor comparison is nominal — stellarator neighbors get no substantive content
- **Target:** Section 7 (Cross-Concept Notes)
- **Category:** analysis
- **Finding:** The analysis correctly names 09-qi-stellarator-hts and 10-large-scale-stellarator as nearest neighbors but provides no substantive comparison against either. Section 7's comparative content is entirely devoted to 21-spherical-tokamak-hts (a spherical tokamak — structurally least similar to Infinity Two). The two structurally closest concepts — Proxima's smaller QI HTS stellarator (R≈1.8 m vs. 12.5 m) and Gauss Fusion's large-scale QI stellarator (LTS+HTS rather than all-HTS) — appear only in the final sentence of Section 7 with no TEA-relevant comparison. The analysis does not address: what does Infinity Two's 7× larger major radius trade against the smaller Proxima design (higher absolute capital for better physics margins and higher thermal output)? Does Gauss Fusion's mixed LTS+HTS approach lower the 3D coil manufacturing TRL risk relative to Infinity Two's all-HTS strategy? These are Goal 1 (positioning) and Goal 3 (TEA implications) questions that the nearest-neighbor section is meant to answer.
- **Recommendation:** Add a paragraph to Section 7 comparing against each named stellarator neighbor: (a) vs. 09-qi-stellarator-hts — what does the large-radius design buy at higher absolute capital: better physics margins, higher thermal output per unit field? How does the REBCO tape demand scale with machine size, and does this reinforce or undermine the cost comparison? (b) vs. 10-large-scale-stellarator — the Gauss Fusion machine uses LTS+HTS rather than all-HTS; does the LTS baseline make the 3D coil manufacturing challenge more tractable (demonstrated LTS winding on complex forms at W7-X scale), and does Infinity Two's all-HTS choice represent a deliberate premium on lower recirculating power at the cost of higher manufacturing TRL risk?
- **Priority:** important

### F-2: Divertor scenario branch called for but only partially implemented in model
- **Target:** model_setup.py / model output
- **Category:** model
- **Finding:** Section 2 explicitly calls for modeling the classical vs. LIBD divertor as a scenario branch: "The divertor design selection should be modeled as a scenario branch: classical (lower capital, availability risk from marginal exhaust) vs. LIBD (higher capital, availability protected by 12.6% exhaust efficiency)." The model implements an availability sweep that captures the operational risk dimension (classical divertor marginal exhaust → lower availability scenario) but does not capture the capital dimension. The LIBD dome adds a novel actively-cooled structure in constrained geometry — higher CAS22 — which has no representation in any model scenario. The current model cannot answer the key trade-off question: does paying the LIBD capital premium buy sufficient LCOE benefit via protected availability to justify the cost?
- **Recommendation:** Add a divertor scenario branch to the model output: a classical scenario (current CAS22 divertor_base cost, availability centered at 80–87%) paired against an LIBD scenario (CAS22 divertor_base multiplied by a plausible range — e.g., 1.5×–2.5× — reflecting the dome and active cooling structure cost premium, availability centered at 87–93%). Report the resulting LCOE pairs in a table analogous to the coil cost and availability sweeps. Even rough capital adder bounds (+$150–400M to CAS22) would make the trade-off quantitatively explicit and testable. The divertor choice is explicitly identified in Section 6 (Gap #17) as scenario-determining with blocking criticality — the model should reflect that framing.
- **Priority:** important

### F-3: Construction time scenario sweep missing despite being the third-highest LCOE sensitivity
- **Target:** model_setup.py / model output
- **Category:** model
- **Finding:** Section 2 identifies three parameters with the greatest LCOE leverage and states explicitly: "These three parameters should be modeled as uncertainty ranges, not point estimates." The three are (1) 3D HTS coil cost (elasticity ≈ +0.99), (2) availability (elasticity ≈ −0.93), (3) construction schedule (elasticity ≈ +0.55). The model sweeps parameters 1 and 2 across named scenarios. Parameter 3 appears in the model output as "10 yr [UNCERTAIN — 3D HTS coil complexity]" with no range explored. The analysis narrative describes schedule overrun from 3D HTS coil manufacturing as "the dominant financial cost driver during construction" and notes no Infinity Two-scale coil has been manufactured — yet the financial impact of a 13- or 16-year construction timeline is invisible in the model output. With elasticity +0.55, a 16-year vs. 10-year construction timeline changes LCOE by roughly 33% — a comparable swing to the availability sweep range (279–329 $/MWh at 350 MWe).
- **Recommendation:** Add a construction time scenario sweep to the model output alongside the existing coil cost and availability sweeps: e.g., 8 yr (optimistic) / 10 yr (central, current default) / 13 yr (schedule overrun, single HTS coil manufacturing delay) / 16 yr (major delay, design iteration required after Infinity One results). Report the resulting LCOE range to make the three-parameter uncertainty triangle — coil cost × availability × schedule — fully visible and consistent with the Section 2 recommendation that all three be modeled as ranges.
- **Priority:** important
