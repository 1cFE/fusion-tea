# Synthesis: Spherical Tokamak - HTS (D-T)

## 1. Executive Summary

- **Critical risk**: Pulsed operation with 15+ minute cycles creates an unmodeled capital cost (thermal energy storage buffer) and reduces availability below steady-state tokamaks—this disadvantage compounds with already-uncompetitive LCOE at 180 $/MWh (600 MWe) and 138 $/MWh (1 GW).

- **Primary advantage**: Complete HTS magnet system validated at tokamak scale (Demo4, 11.8 T) demonstrates that REBCO tape engineering at lower field (5.25 T on-axis) is credible—this is less technically demanding than CFS's 12+ T center post, though the compact center stack shielding challenge remains unique.

- **LCOE**: Model yields 180 $/MWh at native 600 MWe and 138 $/MWh scaled to 1 GW. Both figures assume framework defaults for all capital costs (no published ST-E1 cost data), steam Rankine at 35% thermal efficiency, and 80% availability. The thermal buffer system cost is not modeled—a downward bias of unknown magnitude.

- **Confidence**: **Medium**. Four machine parameters anchor geometry and field strength, but Q value, fusion power, thermal efficiency, auxiliary heating power, and all cost structure are either proprietary or undisclosed. The model rests on analogues from conventional tokamak studies and STEP-related ST research. Analogue quality is reasonable, but three unanchored assumptions in the power balance chain create wide fusion power uncertainty (1.5–2.5 GW estimated from 450–750 MWe net).

## 2. What Matters Most for LCOE

Model sensitivities (elasticity = %LCOE / %parameter):

**1. Availability: -0.88**
- Assumed value: 80%, based on D-T MCF literature range (75–90%, Araiinejad & Shirvan 2025) adjusted downward for pulsed operation
- Source: No published target from Tokamak Energy; DPP 2025 notes "demonstrated compatibility with reactor-level availability factor" but gives no number
- What flips the conclusion: If availability falls to 70% (plausible given dwell periods for CS re-magnetization and pulse-restart stress on divertor), LCOE rises ~12% to 202 $/MWh. If availability reaches 90% (optimistic for pulsed), LCOE falls ~11% to 161 $/MWh. Each 10 percentage points of availability is worth ~18 $/MWh of LCOE. This is the dominant economic lever and also the least constrained by published data.

**2. Major radius (R0): +0.44**
- Assumed value: 5.0 m (published)
- Source: DPP 2025 abstract, confirmed high-confidence parameter
- What flips the conclusion: A 10% increase in R0 (to 5.5 m, driven by blanket or shielding thickness requirements) raises LCOE by 4.4% to 188 $/MWh. This parameter is published and unlikely to change unless the design reverts—but the dramatic evolution from R=4.25 m (2024) to R=5.0 m (2025) shows the design is not frozen. Further growth remains possible.

**3. Interest rate: +0.71**
- Assumed value: 7% (framework default)
- Source: Standard project finance assumption
- What flips the conclusion: A drop to 5% (optimistic green-energy financing) reduces LCOE by 14% to 155 $/MWh. An increase to 9% (reflecting D-T regulatory risk or construction-time uncertainty) raises LCOE by 14% to 205 $/MWh. This parameter encodes regulatory and financial risk that is entirely exogenous to the physics or engineering—it reflects whether D-T fusion is treated like nuclear fission (high cost of capital) or like advanced energy infrastructure (moderate cost).

**4. Thermal efficiency (η_th): -0.32**
- Assumed value: 35% (standardized from 33% steam Rankine analogue per canonical η_th table)
- Source: STEP-related ST research evaluates steam Rankine (30–38%), hybrid ORC, and sCO2 Brayton; ST-E1 cycle not committed
- What flips the conclusion: If ST-E1 commits to sCO2 Brayton at 48% efficiency (a 37% increase), LCOE drops by 12% to 159 $/MWh. If the cycle is a saturated steam Rankine at 32% (8% decrease), LCOE rises by 3% to 186 $/MWh. The cycle choice is worth 20–30 $/MWh of LCOE and is currently undisclosed—this makes it a high-value target for near-term design clarification.

**5. Blanket unit cost: +0.29**
- Assumed value: Framework default for D-T liquid-metal blanket
- Source: No published ST-E1 blanket cost; framework default is anchored to FLiBe immersion blanket literature, not liquid Li outboard-only geometry
- What flips the conclusion: The outboard-only blanket is both a cost advantage (50% coverage vs. 4π) and a cost disadvantage (higher Li-6 enrichment and thicker geometry to achieve TBR=1.2 with limited solid angle). If the ST-E1 blanket costs 30% more per unit volume than a conventional tokamak blanket (plausible given liquid Li reactivity and enrichment), LCOE rises by 9% to 196 $/MWh. If it costs 30% less (optimistic), LCOE falls by 9% to 164 $/MWh. This parameter is entirely unanchored—no ST-specific liquid Li blanket cost data exists in the public domain.

## 3. Risk Verdicts

### Challenge 1: Unknown Q and fusion power—no physics anchor
**Verdict:** Likely resolvable

**Rationale:** Net electric output of 450–750 MWe is published; burning plasma is implicitly required to achieve this range. The absence of Q and fusion power is a transparency gap, not a physics uncertainty. PROCESS or similar system code can generate self-consistent design points for the published geometry (R=5.0 m, A=2.3, B=5.25 T).

**What retires this risk:** Tokamak Energy publishes Q and fusion power, or ST80-HTS demonstrates high-temperature burning plasma (>100M°C achieved transiently in ST40, 2022). ITER Q≥10 demonstration in 2030s provides external validation of burning plasma at tokamak scale, reducing extrapolation uncertainty for any D-T tokamak variant.

---

### Challenge 2: Outboard-only blanket—TBR=1.2 with 50% solid-angle coverage
**Verdict:** Likely resolvable

**Rationale:** TBR=1.2 is achievable with Li-6 enrichment and sufficient blanket thickness (confirmed by neutronics in the 2019 Humphry-Baker & Smith paper for a smaller device). The physics is credible. The engineering challenge is liquid Li chemical reactivity (requires inert atmosphere), tritium extraction from Li metal (less mature than FLiBe extraction), and tritium permeation through structural materials. These are solvable problems with sufficient development effort, and Pb-17Li breeder programs (EU-DEMO TBM) provide partial analogues.

**What retires this risk:** ITER TBM program demonstrates TBR >1.0 in a liquid-metal breeder at realistic neutron flux and duty cycle. Tokamak Energy or STEP program publishes a validated neutronics model for an outboard-only ST blanket showing TBR >1.0 with realistic port fractions and penetrations. Liquid Li extraction system achieves kg/day tritium throughput in a pilot-scale test loop.

---

### Challenge 3: ECRH-only flat-top—high recirculating power
**Verdict:** Likely resolvable

**Rationale:** Gyrotron wall-plug efficiency of 50–55% is lower than NBI (60–70%) or ICRF (~70%), creating higher recirculating power for the same current drive. However, ECRH avoids NBI-induced first-wall erosion and rotation drive, simplifying plasma control. The EPJ 2026 paper (Alieva et al.) confirms that O-mode ECCD is sufficient for flat-top current drive in ST-E1 plasma scenarios. The recirculating power fraction is higher than for bootstrap-current-dominated designs, but not prohibitively so—Q_eng=4.93 in the model is adequate for net electric output.

**What retires this risk:** Tokamak Energy publishes auxiliary heating power for ST-E1, confirming Q_eng >3. ITER or another burning plasma experiment demonstrates sustained ECCD in a high-temperature plasma at the efficiency predicted by ray-tracing codes. CW gyrotrons achieve 60% wall-plug efficiency at multi-MW scale (a 15% efficiency gain reduces recirculating power proportionally).

---

### Challenge 4: Pulsed operation—thermal buffer and availability penalty
**Verdict:** Genuinely uncertain

**Rationale:** 15+ minute pulses with dwell periods for CS re-magnetization create two unmodeled costs: (1) thermal energy storage buffer (molten salt or equivalent) to smooth grid output between pulses, and (2) reduced availability due to pulse-restart stress on divertor and first wall. The DPP 2025 abstract states that pulsed operation is "more desirable than steady-state" for ST geometry due to limited CS flux, but this is a physics argument—it does not address the economic penalty. Molten salt TES is commercially mature (CSP industry), but integration with a fusion heat source at 450–750 MWe scale and sizing for ST-E1 pulse characteristics (undisclosed) are uncharacterized. The availability assumption of 80% is conservative relative to 85–90% for steady-state D-T tokamaks, but the true penalty is unknown.

**What retires this risk:** ST80-HTS or ST-E1 demonstrates multi-hour integrated operation with pulse cycles and validates availability >85%. Tokamak Energy publishes TES system design and cost, or STEP program publishes a TES-integrated pulsed tokamak economic study. Alternatively, the design shifts to quasi-steady operation with bootstrap current dominance, eliminating the pulse-dwell cycle (would require major redesign, unlikely given explicit pulsed-operation advocacy in the Gryaznevich 2023 paper).

---

### Challenge 5: Center stack durability—WC cermet irradiation under 14 MeV neutrons
**Verdict:** Likely resolvable

**Rationale:** WC-FeCr cermet has 10× the flexural strength of pure tungsten and 3–4× better thermal shock resistance, making it the optimal center-stack shielding material on mechanical grounds. However, the 2019 Humphry-Baker & Smith paper explicitly flags that irradiation damage behavior under fusion-relevant 14 MeV neutrons is "not well characterized." Fission-spectrum irradiation data exist for WC but require spectrum unfolding. This is a materials qualification gap, not a fundamental physics barrier—irradiation campaigns can address it. The ~32 cm radial shielding envelope is tight but credible (neutron transport modeling predicts ~1.4 × 10¹⁷ m⁻² s⁻¹ fast neutron flux into the SC core, within REBCO tape tolerance if displacement damage and He production are managed).

**What retires this risk:** A dedicated 14 MeV neutron irradiation campaign (e.g., at IFMIF-DONES or equivalent facility) characterizes WC cermet swelling, embrittlement, and tritium trapping at fusion-relevant fluences. REBCO tape manufacturers publish validated neutron flux tolerance limits for critical current degradation. Tokamak Energy or STEP program demonstrates a center-stack assembly integrating WC shield with HTS coils and validates thermal-mechanical performance under cyclic operation.

---

### Challenge 6: Capital cost structure—no published cost estimate
**Verdict:** Likely resolvable

**Rationale:** This is a transparency gap, not a fundamental uncertainty. The spherical tokamak cost structure is studied in the literature (Brown 2018 IEEE comparative ST/tokamak/stellarator cost decomposition; Foster et al. 2024 UKAEA cost extrapolation framework; Hidalgo-Salaverri 2025 ST TEA with cost-driver sensitivity). The ST-E1 geometry differs from these references (larger machine, REBCO HTS vs. LTS/copper, liquid Li vs. solid breeder), but the cost account categories (magnets, blanket, vessel, divertor, BOP) are universal. Framework defaults produce a credible first-order estimate; concept-specific adjustments (outboard-only blanket reduces cost, WC cermet shield may increase cost, TES buffer is unmodeled) refine it.

**What retires this risk:** Tokamak Energy publishes a cost estimate or partners with UKAEA to produce an ST-E1-specific PROCESS model run. STEP program publishes detailed capital cost breakdown that can be scaled to ST-E1 geometry. Foster et al. 2024 framework is applied to ST-E1 Rev D parameters in a peer-reviewed follow-on study.

## 4. Structural Advantages and Disadvantages

### Advantages relative to conventional D-T tokamak baseline

**1. Lower magnetic field stress**
5.25 T on-axis (vs. 9–12 T for CFS ARC/SPARC) reduces mechanical stress on HTS coils and reduces REBCO tape critical-current requirements per unit length. Demo4 validated 11.8 T at-coil field, which is consistent with 5.25 T on-axis after 1/R falloff—this is a less aggressive operating point than CFS's 20 T demonstration coil. Lower field also reduces magnet quench energy and simplifies quench protection.

**Quantified benefit:** The lower field does not directly reduce magnet capital cost in proportion (the ST-E1 major radius R=5.0 m is 50% larger than ARC's R=3.3 m, so total magnet volume is larger), but it reduces tape performance risk and may enable earlier supply-chain scale-up.

**2. Higher beta plasma**
Spherical tokamak geometry enables β ~30–40% (vs. ~5% for conventional aspect ratio), allowing higher fusion power per unit magnetic energy stored. This partially compensates for the lower field and creates a potentially favorable trade-off between magnet cost and fusion power density. Brown 2018 IEEE cost comparison shows STs can achieve lower $/W_fusion than conventional tokamaks at equivalent magnetic field strength.

**Quantified benefit:** Not directly calculable without fusion power and magnet cost, but the physics basis is credible. The higher beta also improves bootstrap current fraction, reducing auxiliary heating requirements relative to a low-beta tokamak at the same Q.

**3. No beryllium in blanket**
Liquid Li blanket (pure Li metal, not FLiBe) uses no beryllium, eliminating one supply-chain bottleneck. Beryllium is produced at ~300 tonnes/year globally (dominated by Materion Corp.), and FLiBe-based designs (ARC, ARIES-AT) face beryllium supply constraints for fleet scaling. Liquid Li is commercially available at scale (battery-industry demand), and Li-6 enrichment is the only specialized processing step.

**Quantified benefit:** Eliminates ~10–20% of blanket material cost relative to FLiBe (beryllium component), but liquid Li requires inert atmosphere handling and more complex tritium extraction, partially offsetting the advantage.

---

### Disadvantages relative to conventional D-T tokamak baseline

**1. Outboard-only blanket—higher TBR sensitivity to port fractions**
4π blanket coverage (conventional tokamak) tolerates larger diagnostic and maintenance port fractions while maintaining TBR >1.0. Outboard-only coverage at ~50% solid angle requires higher breeding efficiency per unit area, achieved through Li-6 enrichment and thicker blanket geometry. This creates higher sensitivity to local coverage gaps (divertor openings, ECRH launcher access, diagnostics). TBR=1.2 provides margin, but the margin is tighter than for a 4π blanket at TBR=1.15.

**Quantified penalty:** If realistic port fractions reduce effective coverage from 50% to 45% (10% reduction), TBR drops from 1.2 to ~1.1 (assuming linear scaling). This is still self-sufficient, but the margin for uncertainty shrinks. A 4π blanket can absorb a 10% coverage reduction with TBR dropping from 1.15 to 1.04—both remain adequate.

**2. Pulsed operation—thermal buffer capital cost and availability penalty**
15+ minute pulses with dwell periods require thermal energy storage (molten salt or equivalent) to maintain steady grid output. This is a capital cost category absent from steady-state designs. Molten salt TES costs ~$15–30/kWh_th in CSP applications. For a 600 MWe plant at 35% thermal efficiency, thermal power is ~1700 MW_th. Buffering a 5-minute dwell requires ~140 MWh_th of storage, costing $2–4M at CSP pricing. This is <1% of total capital ($7234M in the model), but the true dwell duration and buffer sizing are undisclosed.

**Quantified penalty:** $2–4M for TES (conservative estimate) is negligible. The availability penalty is more consequential: if pulsed operation reduces availability from 85% (steady-state baseline) to 80% (assumed), LCOE rises by ~6% relative to an otherwise-identical steady-state design. This is worth ~11 $/MWh.

**3. Center stack shielding—unique maintenance and replacement challenge**
The compact center stack (~32 cm radial shielding) requires WC cermet material (not standard in tokamak designs) and creates a unique remote-maintenance challenge. The center stack is not modular in the same way as outboard blanket segments—replacement requires disassembly of TF coils or access through limited radial clearance. This adds maintenance complexity and may extend replacement cycles, reducing availability.

**Quantified penalty:** Not calculable without ST-E1 maintenance design, but the DPP 2025 abstract notes that "maintenance scheme and its implications on other systems was an early-stage priority," suggesting Tokamak Energy is aware of the challenge. If center-stack replacement adds 2 weeks per event and occurs every 5 years, availability drops by ~0.5 percentage points—a ~0.6% LCOE penalty (~1 $/MWh). This is small if the replacement schedule is sparse, but cumulative if replacements are frequent.

**4. Larger machine size for equivalent net electric output**
ST-E1 at 600 MWe net has R=5.0 m (plasma major radius), larger than CFS SPARC at ~140 MWe net with R=1.85 m. Scaling to equivalent output (600 MWe), ARC (CFS commercial design) has R=3.3 m. The ST-E1 major radius is 50% larger than ARC for similar output, implying higher building and reactor plant equipment costs per unit output. The model's 1 GW scaled LCOE (138 $/MWh) is higher than typical tokamak scaling (ARIES-AT projects 80–100 $/MWh at 1 GW), suggesting the cost per unit output does not improve as strongly with scale for the ST geometry.

**Quantified penalty:** The 1 GW LCOE of 138 $/MWh (vs. 180 $/MWh at 600 MWe) shows 23% LCOE reduction from a 67% power increase—this is weaker than the 0.7–0.8 power-law exponent typical of tokamak plant studies (which would predict ~30% LCOE reduction). The ST geometry's cost scaling is less favorable than conventional aspect-ratio tokamaks.

## 5. Cross-Concept Positioning

**Spherical tokamak position in the fusion landscape:**

Tokamak Energy sits in the **magnetic confinement, D-T fuel, HTS magnet** cluster alongside Commonwealth Fusion Systems (CFS). Both concepts use REBCO HTS to achieve compact geometry and burning plasma at pilot-plant scale. The key architectural difference is **aspect ratio**: CFS pursues high field at conventional aspect ratio (A~3.0, B~12 T), while Tokamak Energy pursues lower field at low aspect ratio (A~2.3, B~5.25 T), compensating with high beta.

**What makes ST-E1 fundamentally different:**
1. **Asymmetric blanket/shielding**: Outboard-only liquid Li blanket vs. CFS FLiBe immersion with 4π coverage. This changes tritium breeding sensitivity, blanket cost structure, and supply-chain material requirements (no beryllium).
2. **Pulsed operation**: ST-E1 embraces 15+ minute pulses as "more desirable" for ST geometry, while CFS targets quasi-steady operation. This adds thermal buffer cost and availability penalty unique to ST-E1.
3. **Lower field, larger machine**: 5.25 T on-axis at R=5.0 m vs. ARC 9.2 T at R=3.3 m. Lower field reduces magnet stress but increases machine size for equivalent output.

**Concepts sharing similar economics:**
- **CFS ARC/SPARC**: Same D-T fuel cycle, same HTS magnet supply-chain bottleneck (REBCO tape), same tritium self-sufficiency challenge. Both face regulatory cost uncertainty (Stewart & Shirvan 2.2× building cost multiplier for fission-style regulation). Both target mid-2030s pilot plants. LCOE drivers (availability, interest rate, blanket cost) are identical. The primary difference is magnetic architecture (high-field conventional vs. low-field spherical).

- **STEP (UKAEA spherical tokamak)**: Direct ST-family analogue. STEP targets larger scale (~500 MWe net in early concepts) and similar pulsed-operation philosophy. Hidalgo-Salaverri 2025 ST TEA provides the closest published economic study. STEP evaluates steam Rankine, hybrid ORC, and sCO2 Brayton for power conversion—same options as ST-E1. Cost structure and availability challenges are nearly identical.

- **ITER (if considered a pilot plant)**: Same D-T fuel cycle, same tokamak physics regime, same regulatory challenges. ITER is much larger (R=6.2 m, 500 MW fusion but not net-electric), superconducting magnets (Nb₃Sn, not HTS), and not optimized for LCOE. ITER's TBR~1.15 (via TBM program) and tritium self-sufficiency target parallels ST-E1's TBR=1.2.

**Where ST-E1 diverges from the tokamak mainstream:**
- Pulsed operation creates an economic structure closer to **MAST-U / NSTX-U** (experimental STs) than to steady-state tokamak power plants (ARC, DEMO). The thermal buffer and availability penalty are unique to pulsed concepts.
- Lower field (5.25 T) places ST-E1 in a less aggressive operating regime than CFS (12+ T center post) or ITER (11.8 T at coil). This reduces magnet risk but increases machine size and may reduce competitiveness on $/W_fusion.

## 6. Modeling Confidence

**Rating: Medium**

### Anchored parameters (4 of 13 LCOE-critical inputs)
- Geometry: R=5.0 m, A=2.3 → minor radius 2.17 m (published, high confidence)
- Magnetic field: B=5.25 T on-axis (published, high confidence)
- Net electric output: 600 MWe central of 450–750 MWe range (published, medium confidence—wide range reflects design uncertainty)
- Blanket concept: outboard-only liquid Li, TBR=1.2 (published, high confidence on concept, low confidence on engineering realization)

### Speculative parameters (9 of 13 LCOE-critical inputs)
- **Q value / fusion power**: Entirely derived from net electric output, assuming thermal efficiency and recirculating fraction. Model produces Q_eng=4.93 and P_fusion=1944 MW, both unverified. Uncertainty band is ±30% (fusion power range 1.4–2.5 GW from the 450–750 MWe net output range).
- **Thermal efficiency**: 35% assumed (standardized from 33% steam Rankine analogue per canonical η_th table), but cycle not committed by Tokamak Energy. Could be 32% (saturated steam) to 48% (sCO2 Brayton). LCOE elasticity is -0.32, so 33% → 48% efficiency improvement (45% change) drops LCOE by 14% to 155 $/MWh.
- **Auxiliary heating power**: 50 MW framework default, undisclosed by Tokamak Energy. True value derivable from Q once published. LCOE elasticity is +0.06 (low sensitivity), but recirculating power fraction is a key engineering metric.
- **Availability**: 80% assumed, no published target. Pulsed operation suggests lower availability than 85–90% steady-state baseline. LCOE elasticity is -0.88 (dominant sensitivity). If availability is actually 70%, LCOE rises to 202 $/MWh (+12%).
- **Capital costs (all CAS accounts)**: Framework defaults for every account. No published cost estimate for ST-E1. Blanket cost assumes 4π coverage (overestimate for outboard-only geometry, conservative bias). Thermal buffer (molten salt TES) is not modeled—a downward bias of ~$2–4M (~0.05% of capital).
- **Elongation**: 2.5 assumed (typical for ST at A~2.3), unpublished for ST-E1. LCOE elasticity is +0.44 (high sensitivity), but elongation is constrained by vertical stability—unlikely to vary by more than ±20%.

### Dominant source of LCOE uncertainty
**Availability and capacity factor**—the model assumes 80% availability, but pulsed operation (dwell periods, pulse-restart stress, thermal buffer integration) creates uncertainty in this parameter. The published 450–750 MWe range reflects both physics uncertainty (Q, confinement quality) and technology uncertainty (component lifetimes, maintenance schedules). Availability has elasticity -0.88, making it the single largest driver of LCOE spread. A 10-percentage-point availability swing (70% to 80% or 80% to 90%) changes LCOE by ±18 $/MWh—comparable to the entire uncertainty from capital cost structure.

The second-largest uncertainty is **capital cost**: all CAS accounts use framework defaults because no ST-E1-specific cost data exist. The framework defaults are anchored to conventional tokamak studies (ARIES, EUROfusion DEMO) and may not capture ST-specific cost advantages (outboard-only blanket, no beryllium) or disadvantages (WC cermet shield, thermal buffer). Blanket unit cost has elasticity +0.29, so a 30% error in blanket cost translates to ~9% LCOE error (~16 $/MWh).

## 7. What Would Change My Mind

**1. ST80-HTS demonstrates integrated pulsed operation with availability >85% over months of operation**
ST80-HTS (completion target ~2026) is the first HTS spherical tokamak at significant scale. If ST80-HTS achieves multi-month campaigns with pulse cycles (15+ minute burns, dwell periods, repeat) and sustained availability >85%, the pulsed-operation penalty is resolved as negligible. This would retire the primary economic disadvantage and improve the LCOE estimate by ~6% (~11 $/MWh downward).

If ST80-HTS instead demonstrates availability <75% due to pulse-restart stress, divertor degradation, or thermal transients, the LCOE estimate rises by ~12% (~22 $/MWh upward) and the concept becomes uncompetitive even at 1 GW scale.

**2. Tokamak Energy publishes Q >15 and fusion power >2 GW for ST-E1 Rev D**
The model assumes Q_eng~5 (derived from 600 MWe net at 35% thermal efficiency). If Tokamak Energy discloses Q >15 (burning plasma with very low auxiliary heating), recirculating power fraction drops substantially, net electric output rises for the same fusion power, and LCOE improves. A Q=20 plasma (vs. Q_eng=4.93 assumed) at the same fusion power would increase net output by ~10–15%, improving LCOE by ~10% (~18 $/MWh downward).

If Q <10 (lower than expected for a burning plasma), auxiliary heating power is higher than assumed, recirculating fraction rises, and LCOE worsens by ~10–15% (~20 $/MWh upward). This would place ST-E1 LCOE above 200 $/MWh even at 1 GW scale.

**3. STEP program publishes a spherical tokamak LCOE study showing <100 $/MWh at 1 GW scale with pulsed operation**
STEP (UKAEA) is the closest public-domain analogue to ST-E1. If STEP produces a detailed techno-economic study showing that pulsed-ST economics are competitive with conventional tokamaks (<100 $/MWh at NOAK 1 GW), it validates the ST-E1 cost structure and suggests the framework defaults used here are overly conservative. This would shift the ST-E1 1 GW LCOE estimate downward by ~20–30% (to ~100–110 $/MWh), placing it in the competitive range for baseload power.

If STEP instead projects >150 $/MWh at 1 GW for a pulsed ST, it confirms that the ST geometry's cost scaling is unfavorable and ST-E1's 138 $/MWh (1 GW) is optimistic. This would shift the estimate upward by ~10–15% (to ~150–160 $/MWh), reinforcing uncompetitiveness.

## 8. LCOE Downselect Scoring

### Overview

This concept scores well on **modularization (C1)** and **supply chain learning (C3)** due to the large fraction of conventional components (steam cycle BOP, steel structures, pumps) and elimination of beryllium supply constraints. It scores poorly on **plant complexity (C4)** due to operational coupling between pulsed plasma, thermal buffer, and divertor/first-wall wear, and on **customization needs (C5)** due to D-T fuel handling and large cooling towers for steam Rankine. **Technical risk (C7)** is elevated for plasma-wall interaction (asymmetric divertor heat loads in ST geometry) and fuel cycle closure (liquid Li tritium extraction immaturity), but physics performance and instability control benefit from substantial ST heritage (MAST-U, NSTX-U, ST40).
