### F-1: Tritium surplus as byproduct revenue stream — omitted from TEA analysis
- **Target:** Section 2 (Challenge 4: Liquid Lithium Chamber / TEA implications) and Section 5 (LCOE-Relevant Parameters)
- **Finding:** The analysis establishes TBR 1.8 and 25 kg/year tritium surplus as a fleet-deployment advantage (reduced startup constraint), but never quantifies the economic value of that surplus as a byproduct revenue stream. The Engineer (theengineer) article establishes tritium market value at ~$30,000/g — consistent with the $30,000/g figure the analysis already uses for startup inventory cost. At 25 kg/year surplus and $30,000/g, this represents ~$750M/year in potential byproduct revenue for a 333 MWe plant. A plant at $50/MWh and 85% capacity factor generates approximately $1.25B/year in electricity revenue. Tritium byproduct revenue could approach 60% of electricity revenue — a TEA factor of first order if credited, and a material risk if tritium prices collapse as fusion scales (which they would). The analysis's modeling recommendations (Section 2, Hypotheses 1–4) do not include a tritium revenue scenario branch. This is a complete omission relevant to Goal 3 (TEA implications) and Goal 4 (modeling approach).
- **Recommendation:** Add a paragraph to Section 2's discussion of the liquid lithium curtain advantage explaining the revenue duality: the 25 kg/year tritium surplus is simultaneously a fleet-deployment enabler and a potential byproduct revenue stream of ~$750M/year at current tritium scarcity pricing (~$30k/g). Flag that this revenue is contingent on tritium price holding — a price that would collapse if fusion fleets deploy (closing the loop: FLF's surplus advantage is self-eroding at scale). Add a row to the Section 5 Available Parameters table for "Tritium surplus revenue" (value: ~$750M/year at 333 MWe; source: theengineer-content-news article / $30k/g × 25 kg/yr; confidence: low — price assumption is speculative). Add Hypothesis 5 to the Section 2 modeling hypotheses: *If tritium surplus revenue is credited, LCOE drops by X $/MWh — test as a scenario branch rather than a base case assumption, and include sensitivity to tritium price from $0/g (fleet saturation) to $30,000/g (current scarcity).*
- **Priority:** important

### F-2: Lithium enrichment capital cost data — fills listed gap #9
- **Target:** Section 4 (Key Materials and Supply Chain) and Section 6 (Data Gap Inventory, gap #9)
- **Finding:** The analysis states in Section 4 that "FLF's public materials do not address enrichment requirements — a notable gap" and lists "⁶Li enrichment fraction required for TBR 1.8" as gap #9 (derivable, important). The IP Group portfolio news article (ipgroupplc, September 2025) provides actual cost data from FLF's white paper: natural lithium costs ~$70M per reactor; enriched lithium alternatives cost $143M–$451M per reactor. This 2–6× cost premium for enriched Li is directly relevant to the CAS account for special materials (CAS27) and the supply chain risk narrative. The analysis identifies ⁶Li enrichment capacity as "a supply chain risk for a fleet of plants" but has no cost figures attached.
- **Recommendation:** Update Section 4 (Lithium-6 Enrichment paragraph) to include the white-paper cost figures: natural Li ~$70M/reactor vs. enriched Li ~$143–$451M/reactor, citing ipgroupplc-news-2025-09-19. Note that FLF's design appears to use natural lithium (consistent with the $70M figure being the reference case) — if so, the TBR 1.8 claim with natural lithium is even more significant, as most ICF blanket designs achieving high TBR require enriched ⁶Li. Update Section 5 Missing Parameters to move ⁶Li enrichment from "derivable" to "partially sourced" and add the $70M / $143–$451M range. Update Section 6 gap #9 to reflect this partial data.
- **Priority:** important

### F-3: Machine 3 neutron yield (~50 neutrons) — quantifies the physics gap
- **Target:** Section 1 (Availability of Data) and Section 3 (Target Physics / High-Gain Compression subsystem)
- **Finding:** The analysis notes that Machine 3 produced fusion neutrons confirmed by UKAEA in 2022 but states "the gain achieved has not been published." The PR Newswire announcement (prnewswire, April 2022) discloses that Machine 3 produced approximately 50 neutrons. This is a public datum that partially fills the gap: while it is not a gain ratio, a yield of ~50 neutrons at 6.5 km/s places the 2022 result at a negligible fraction of breakeven (NIF's ignition result in December 2022 used ~3 MJ to produce 3.15 MJ, with a neutron yield orders of magnitude higher). The analysis's characterization of gain as "almost certainly far below commercial thresholds" is correct but understates the scale — the gap from 50 neutrons to 200× gain at 60 km/s is an extrapolation over many orders of magnitude. The same source discloses the total development investment to reach the 2022 milestone: <£45M (~$57M), which is useful benchmarking context not present in the analysis.
- **Recommendation:** In Section 1, add a sentence noting that the 2022 Machine 3 result produced approximately 50 neutrons (sourced from prnewswire April 2022 announcement), and note the total development cost to reach that milestone was <£45M. In Section 3 (Target Physics TRL paragraph), add one sentence: the ~50-neutron yield establishes that the 2022 demonstration was deeply sub-ignition — not merely "below commercial thresholds" but many orders of magnitude from them — which reinforces the TRL 3–4 rating and the commercial binary threshold framing. Update Section 6 gap #2 ("gain achieved in 2022 demonstration") to note the neutron count is public (~50 neutrons per prnewswire); the actual gain ratio remains proprietary but can be bounded from below as essentially zero.
- **Priority:** minor
