### F-1: CAS25 power conversion direction uses wrong framing
- **Target:** Section 7 (CAS-Level Cost Delta table, CAS25 row)
- **Finding:** The analysis labels CAS25/Power Conversion as "Small −" (Stellaris cheaper) but the reasoning is internally contradictory. The stated argument — "Lower efficiency means less gross power from the same thermal plant, reducing BOP capital cost per MWe of thermal input" — uses per-MWth as the denominator. All cross-concept comparisons normalize to 1 GWe net output. At fixed net output, 32% thermal efficiency requires ~3.1 GWth input vs. ~2.5 GWth for a 40%-efficient reference, which means a *larger* steam plant, not smaller. The word "paradoxically" in the notes signals the author recognized this tension but did not resolve it. The direction "Small −" may be correct — steam Rankine is a cheaper technology per unit thermal capacity than sCO₂ Brayton — but this argument is buried in the last sentence rather than being the primary case. The muddled reasoning creates a misleading impression that lower efficiency is itself a cost advantage.
- **Recommendation:** Replace the current reasoning with the direct argument: water Rankine at ~500°C is a significantly cheaper technology per unit thermal capacity than sCO₂ Brayton at higher temperatures (lower capital cost per GWth, mature supply chain). This technology discount partially offsets the higher thermal throughput required at 32% efficiency vs. 40%. State explicitly whether the net direction is "Small −" or "0" and quantify the competing effects rather than relying on the current "paradoxically negative" framing.
- **Priority:** important

### F-2: Coil cost ranked #1 sensitivity axis, but model elasticities show availability has ~10× higher leverage
- **Target:** Section 2 (Recommended Modeling Approach, primary sensitivity axes)
- **Finding:** Section 2 ranks the 3D coil cost multiplier as the "#1 sensitivity axis" and calls it "the single parameter that dominates the competitiveness question." The model data does not support this framing. The multiplier sweep shows: going from DEFAULT to 5× C220103 (a 400% increase) moves LCOE from $106.4 to $142.6/MWh — a 34% change, implying an elasticity of ~0.08. The formal sensitivity table shows availability elasticity at −0.89, which is ~10× higher. Coil cost and availability are different *types* of uncertainty being conflated: (1) coil cost is a viability threshold — if the premium exceeds ~2×, stellarator CAPEX likely cannot be competitive regardless of other factors (a binary go/no-go gate); (2) availability is a continuous parameter sensitivity — the primary lever for optimizing LCOE within a viable design. Calling coil cost "#1 sensitivity axis" implies it is the biggest knob for moving LCOE, when in fact availability moves LCOE far more per unit. A reader using the sensitivity axes to prioritize modeling effort would focus on coil cost first when they should focus on availability.
- **Recommendation:** Separate the two roles explicitly in the sensitivity axis description. State that the 3D coil cost multiplier is the primary *viability threshold* — a scenario gate that determines whether the concept is competitive at all — while availability is the primary *continuous sensitivity parameter* that dominates LCOE movement within a viable scenario. The coil multiplier sweep already illustrates this; the narrative framing should match it.
- **Priority:** important
